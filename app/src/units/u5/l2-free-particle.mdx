Throw a tennis ball and it stays a tennis ball. It doesn't get blurrier as it flies. Newton's laws move a point along a trajectory, and that's the end of the story.

A quantum particle flying through empty space is described by a wavefunction, and the Schrödinger equation says how that function evolves. There are no forces and no obstacles, just a free particle. What could possibly happen?

## Plane waves: the free particle's stationary states

For a free particle the only energy is kinetic, $\hat H = \dfrac{\hat p^2}{2m} = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}$. Last lesson you met the momentum eigenstates $e^{ikx}$. They are energy eigenstates too:

$$
\hat H\,e^{ikx} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,e^{ikx}, \qquad E = \frac{p^2}{2m}, \quad p = \hbar k .
$$

From World 4, a stationary state just spins its phase clock at rate $\omega = E/\hbar$. So the plane wave evolves as

$$
\psi_k(x,t) = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}, \qquad \omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}.
$$

That's a travelling wave. The colour pattern slides along at the **phase velocity** $v_\text{phase} = \omega/k = \hbar k/2m$.

Something odd there: the classical velocity of a particle with momentum $p = \hbar k$ is $p/m = \hbar k/m$, which is *twice* the phase velocity. Also, a plane wave isn't localised at all: $|\psi|^2$ is the same everywhere, and it can't even be normalised. Real particles must be something else.

The relation $\omega(k) = \hbar k^2/2m$ is called a **dispersion relation**. Because $\omega$ is not proportional to $k$, waves with different $k$ travel at different speeds. That's what "dispersive" means, and it's the root of everything in this lesson.

## Building a packet

A localised particle is a **superposition of plane waves**, a wave packet:

$$
\psi(x,t) = \int \phi(k)\, e^{i(kx - \omega(k)t)}\,dk .
$$

Here $\phi(k)$ says how much of each momentum is in the mix. Build one yourself:

- Press **▶ evolve**. The red dot rides one crest at the phase velocity. The green triangle tracks the envelope. **Crests slide forward through the envelope**, which moves twice as fast as they do.

The envelope moves at the **group velocity**:

$$
v_\text{group} = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{p}{m}.
$$

That's exactly the classical velocity. The phase velocity has no direct physical meaning: you can't send a signal on a single crest. **The packet is the particle**, and it moves just as Newton said it would.

## The Schrödinger simulator

Now let the exact Schrödinger equation do the work. This simulator stores $\psi$ on 1024 grid points and advances it with the **split-step Fourier method**. Each tiny time step applies the potential's phase in position space, jumps to momentum space with an FFT, applies the kinetic phase $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$, and jumps back. Every piece is a pure phase, so each step is exactly unitary, just like the integrator race in World 4. Units: $\hbar = m = 1$.

<Try>
- Press **▶ play**. The packet glides right at speed $k_0$ (the readout ⟨x⟩ grows by 2 per unit time), and its width $\Delta x$ grows.
- Watch the **momentum panel**: it never changes. With no forces, momentum is conserved, so $|\phi(p)|^2$ is frozen while $|\psi(x)|^2$ spreads.
- Watch the colours. After a while the front of the packet cycles colours faster than the back: fast components have run ahead of slow ones (a "chirp").
- Make $\sigma$ small (0.5) and reset. The packet spreads *much* faster. Make it wide (5): it barely spreads.
- Set $k_0$ negative: it moves left, and the colour cycling reverses direction.
- Press **📍 measure position** mid-flight. The packet collapses to a sharp spike, which then spreads explosively. A narrow position means a huge momentum spread.
</Try>

For a Gaussian packet of initial width $\sigma_0$ there's an exact formula for the width:

$$
\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}.
$$

The spreading timescale is $\tau = 2m\sigma_0^2/\hbar$. It grows with the **square** of the initial width: halve the packet and it spreads four times faster.

## Why baseballs don't blur

So why doesn't the tennis ball spread? Put in real numbers.

- An **electron** localised to $\sigma_0 = 1$ nm: $\tau = 2m_e\sigma_0^2/\hbar \approx 2(9.11\times10^{-31})(10^{-18})/(1.055\times10^{-34}) \approx 1.7\times10^{-14}$ s. In about 17 femtoseconds it has doubled in width.
- A **1 gram marble** localised to $1$ µm: $\tau \approx 2(10^{-3})(10^{-12})/(1.055\times10^{-34}) \approx 2\times10^{19}$ s, more than 40 times the age of the universe.

Spreading scales as $\hbar/m$. For anything you can hold, $\hbar/m$ is absurdly small, so packets stay sharp and Newton's trajectories are an excellent approximation. That's the **classical limit**: quantum mechanics doesn't switch off, its effects just become too small to see.

<NumQuiz id="u5l2-electron-tau" answer={17.3} tol={0.5}>
  <Q>Compute the spreading time $\tau = 2m\sigma_0^2/\hbar$ for an electron with $\sigma_0 = 1$ nm, in **femtoseconds**. ($m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg, $\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-34}$ J·s.)</Q>
  <Explain>$\tau = 2(9.109\times10^{-31})(10^{-18})/(1.0546\times10^{-34}) \approx 1.73\times10^{-14}$ s $= 17.3$ fs.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

<Warning>
**Spreading doesn't mean the particle gets bigger.** The electron itself is still a point-like particle. Measure its position and you get one sharp answer. What spreads is our **uncertainty**, the range of places it could be found. And spreading isn't irreversible information loss: run the Schrödinger equation backwards and the packet re-focuses. (In the box lesson you'll see packets spontaneously re-form.)
</Warning>

<History>
**Louis de Broglie**'s 1924 thesis proposed that matter has a wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, and he already noted that the particle should move at the **group** velocity of its waves. Davisson and Germer confirmed electron waves in 1927. The distinction between group and phase velocity itself is older: Hamilton noticed it in 1839, and Lord Rayleigh popularised it for water and sound waves. Phase velocities can exceed $c$ for some waves without violating relativity, because no information rides on a single crest.
</History>

## Summary

- Free-particle energy eigenstates are plane waves $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ with $p = \hbar k$ and $E = \hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ (dispersive).
- A localised particle is a **wave packet**, a superposition of plane waves.
- The envelope moves at the **group velocity** $d\omega/dk = p/m$, the classical velocity. Crests move at the phase velocity $\omega/k$, which is half that.
- Free packets **spread**: $\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + (\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}$. Narrower packets spread faster. The momentum distribution never changes.
- For macroscopic masses, $\hbar/m$ makes spreading negligible. That's the classical limit.
- The simulator uses the unitary **split-step Fourier** method with FFTs.
