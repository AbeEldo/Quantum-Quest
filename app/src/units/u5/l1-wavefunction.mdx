A qubit has two outcomes, so its state is two amplitudes. A spin-1 particle has three outcomes, so three amplitudes. Now take an electron in a wire and ask the most basic question there is: **where is it?**

The possible answers are every point along the wire: $x = 0.3$, $x = 0.30001$, $x = \pi$. That's a continuum of outcomes. If each outcome needs its own amplitude, the state of a single electron is a list of *uncountably many* complex numbers.

That list is a function, $\psi(x)$, called the **wavefunction**. This lesson shows how the familiar rules (amplitudes, the Born rule, inner products) carry over from $\mathbb{C}^2$ to functions, and how to *see* a complex-valued function.

## From amplitudes to a density

Chop space into $N$ little bins of width $\Delta x$ centred at $x_j$. Then the particle is a perfectly ordinary $N$-outcome quantum system:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{N} c_j\,|x_j\rangle, \qquad \sum_j |c_j|^2 = 1 .
$$

As the bins shrink, each $|c_j|^2$ shrinks in proportion to $\Delta x$. So the sensible thing to track is the amplitude *per square-root of length*, defined by

$$
c_j = \psi(x_j)\,\sqrt{\Delta x}.
$$

Then $\sum_j |c_j|^2 = \sum_j |\psi(x_j)|^2\,\Delta x$, which is a Riemann sum. In the limit it becomes an integral:

<KeyIdea>
**The wavefunction** $\psi(x)$ is a complex-valued function with

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1 \qquad\text{(normalisation)}.
$$

**Born rule for position:** $|\psi(x)|^2$ is a **probability density**, so

$$
P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\,dx .
$$
</KeyIdea>

Watch the limit happen. With few sites you have a handful of amplitude clocks, just like a qudit. Add sites and every bar shrinks, but the bars divided by $\Delta x$ settle onto a smooth curve:

Notice the units: since $\int|\psi|^2dx$ is a pure number, $\psi$ has units of $1/\sqrt{\text{length}}$. That's a quick sanity check on any wavefunction you write down.

## Seeing a complex function

$\psi(x)$ is complex, so at every point it has a **magnitude** and a **phase**. A graph of a complex function needs *three* dimensions: one for $x$ and two for the complex value. Switch the builder to **3D helix** to see it directly: $x$ runs along the axis, and at each point an arrow sticks out to $(\operatorname{Re}\psi, \operatorname{Im}\psi)$.

<Try>
- Choose **wave packet** with $k = 0$. The curve lies flat in one plane: a real, positive bump.
- Increase $k$. The curve becomes a **helix** wrapped around a Gaussian envelope. The phase rotates as you move along $x$.
- Rotate the view. From the side you see $\operatorname{Re}\psi$, a wiggly cosine-like curve. Rotate 90° and you see $\operatorname{Im}\psi$. Neither alone is "the" wavefunction. The helix is.
- Turn on **auto-rotate** and watch the corkscrew turn.
</Try>

Three-dimensional pictures are hard to read at a glance, so this course uses a compact convention everywhere:

<KeyIdea>
**Phase as colour.** Draw $|\psi(x)|^2$ as a filled curve, and colour each slice by the phase $\arg\psi(x)$ using the same colour wheel as the amplitude clocks in earlier worlds. Height tells you *where* the particle is likely to be. Colour tells you the *phase*, which (you'll see next lesson) encodes *how it's moving*.
</KeyIdea>

The most important example is the **plane wave** $\psi(x) = e^{ikx}$. Its magnitude is $1$ everywhere, so $|\psi|^2$ is perfectly flat, and all the information is in the phase. The colours cycle through the rainbow with spatial period $2\pi/k$. Flip the sign, $e^{-ikx}$, and the helix twists the other way. The density is identical, but as you'll see next lesson, the two describe particles moving in **opposite directions**.

<Warning>
**$|\psi|^2$ is not the whole story.** Two wavefunctions with the same density can behave completely differently. $e^{ikx}$ and $e^{-ikx}$ move in opposite directions. Two humps with relative phase $0$ or $\pi$ will interfere differently when they meet. As in World 3, relative phase is physical: it's invisible in one measurement basis (position) but decisive in another (momentum).
</Warning>

## Inner products and operators, continued

Every piece of Dirac notation survives the jump from sums to integrals:

| Discrete ($\mathbb{C}^N$) | Continuous (functions) |
|---|---|
| $\langle\phi\vert\psi\rangle = \sum_j \phi_j^*\psi_j$ | $\langle\phi\vert\psi\rangle = \int \phi^*(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx$ |
| amplitude to find site $j$: $c_j$ | amplitude density at $x$: $\psi(x) = \langle x\vert\psi\rangle$ |
| $\langle A\rangle = \langle\psi\vert\hat A\vert\psi\rangle$ | $\langle A\rangle = \int \psi^*(x)\,\hat A\,\psi(x)\,dx$ |

Two operators matter most:

- **Position** $\hat x$ just multiplies by $x$: $\hat x\,\psi(x) = x\,\psi(x)$. So $\langle x\rangle = \int x\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$, which is the ordinary mean of the density.
- **Momentum** is a derivative: $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\dfrac{d}{dx}$. Test it on a plane wave: $\hat p\,e^{ikx} = \hbar k\,e^{ikx}$. The plane wave is a momentum eigenstate with eigenvalue $p = \hbar k$. That's de Broglie's $p = h/\lambda$ from World 1, now derived as an eigenvalue equation.

Position and momentum don't commute. Apply both orders to any test function $f$:

$$
[\hat x,\hat p]f = x(-i\hbar f') - (-i\hbar)(xf)' = -i\hbar x f' + i\hbar(f + xf') = i\hbar f .
$$

So $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$, which is the commutator behind Heisenberg's relation from World 4. You'll explore it visually in the last lesson of this world.

<CSCorner>
A wavefunction is a vector with a continuum of components, an element of the Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ of square-integrable functions. Computers can't store a continuum, so every simulator does what our widget did: **sample** $\psi$ on a grid of $N$ points. The Schrödinger simulator in the next lesson uses $N = 1024$ complex numbers, which is a 1024-dimensional state vector. That's equivalent to 10 qubits' worth of amplitudes, used to describe *one* particle in *one* dimension. Three particles in 3D would need $1024^{9}$ amplitudes. This blow-up is why Feynman suggested building quantum computers to simulate quantum physics.
</CSCorner>

<DeepDive title="Position eigenstates and the Dirac delta">
What are the eigenvectors of $\hat x$? A state with a perfectly definite position $x_0$ would be $\psi(x) = \delta(x - x_0)$, Dirac's "function" that is zero everywhere except $x_0$ and integrates to 1. It isn't square-integrable ($\int\delta^2 = \infty$), so it's not a physical state, only a useful idealisation. Plane waves $e^{ikx}$ have the same problem: $\int|e^{ikx}|^2dx = \infty$. Real particles are always **wave packets**, superpositions of many such idealised states. Physicists use the "kets" $|x\rangle$ and $|p\rangle$ freely, with orthonormality $\langle x|x'\rangle = \delta(x - x')$ replacing $\delta_{jk}$.
</DeepDive>

<History>
**Schrödinger** first pictured $\psi$ as a real physical wave, with $e|\psi|^2$ the smeared-out charge density of the electron. It didn't work: for $n$ particles, $\psi$ lives in $3n$-dimensional configuration space, not ordinary space. In June 1926 **Max Born**, analysing scattering, proposed that $|\psi|^2$ gives *probabilities*. Schrödinger never fully accepted it; his famous cat (1935) was meant as a reductio ad absurdum.
</History>

## Summary

- A particle's position state is a **wavefunction** $\psi(x) = \langle x|\psi\rangle$, the continuum limit of amplitudes $c_j = \psi(x_j)\sqrt{\Delta x}$.
- $|\psi(x)|^2$ is a **probability density**: $P(a\le x\le b) = \int_a^b|\psi|^2dx$, with $\int|\psi|^2dx = 1$.
- $\psi$ is complex. We draw $|\psi|^2$ coloured by phase, or the full 3D helix.
- Position-dependent phases are physical. Only a global phase is not.
- $\hat x = x$, $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$, and $e^{ikx}$ is a momentum eigenstate with $p = \hbar k$.
- $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$.
