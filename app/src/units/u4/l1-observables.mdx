In World 3 you sent silver atoms through Stern–Gerlach magnets. Turn the magnet to point along $z$ and every atom lands in one of **two** spots. Turn it to point along $x$, or tilt it $37°$ off vertical, and you still get exactly two spots. You never see a smear.

Classically that's very strange. A spinning magnet pointed in a random direction should have *every* possible component along the magnet axis, from fully up to fully down, and the beam should spread into a continuous stripe. Instead nature hands back one of two answers, whatever question you ask.

This world is about the **rules of the game**: the handful of postulates that every quantum system obeys, from a single electron to a 1000-qubit processor. The first rule answers a question we've dodged so far: **what is a measurable quantity, mathematically?**

## Observables are matrices

Here's the idea that Heisenberg, Born and Jordan stumbled onto in 1925, stated in the clean form it reached a few years later:

<KeyIdea>
**Postulate (observables).** Every measurable quantity $A$ is represented by a **Hermitian operator** $\hat A = \hat A^\dagger$ on the state space.

- The **possible outcomes** of measuring $A$ are the **eigenvalues** $a_k$ of $\hat A$.
- Each outcome comes with an **eigenvector** $|a_k\rangle$: a state in which that outcome is *certain*.
</KeyIdea>

In World 0 you met Hermitian matrices and the **spectral theorem**: a Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so it can be written

$$
\hat A \;=\; \sum_k a_k\, |a_k\rangle\langle a_k| .
$$

Read this formula as a **lookup table** packed into one matrix. Each term pairs an outcome (the number $a_k$) with the state that gives it (the projector $|a_k\rangle\langle a_k|$).

Why Hermitian, and not some other kind of matrix? Two physical requirements force it:

1. **Outcomes are real numbers.** Detectors read out real numbers like $+\hbar/2$ or $2.3$ eV. Hermitian matrices are exactly the normal matrices with real eigenvalues.
2. **Different outcomes must be perfectly distinguishable.** If a state *certainly* gives $a_1$, it must *never* give $a_2$. Zero probability means zero overlap: $\langle a_1|a_2\rangle = 0$. Hermitian matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues.

<DeepDive title="Proof: real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors">
Let $\hat A|a\rangle = a|a\rangle$ with $\langle a|a\rangle = 1$. Then $\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle = a$. Take the complex conjugate: $\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle^* = \langle a|\hat A^\dagger|a\rangle = \langle a|\hat A|a\rangle$, because $\hat A^\dagger = \hat A$. So $a^* = a$, which means $a$ is real.

Now take two eigenvectors with $a_1 \ne a_2$. Compute $\langle a_1|\hat A|a_2\rangle$ two ways. Acting to the right gives $a_2\langle a_1|a_2\rangle$. Acting to the left (using $\hat A^\dagger = \hat A$ and $a_1$ real) gives $a_1\langle a_1|a_2\rangle$. Subtracting, $(a_1 - a_2)\langle a_1|a_2\rangle = 0$, so $\langle a_1|a_2\rangle = 0$. ∎
</DeepDive>

## The Pauli matrices: spin, built from experiments

Let's *build* the spin observables directly from the Stern–Gerlach data, using the lookup-table formula. We'll measure spin in units of $\hbar/2$, so the outcomes are $\pm 1$.

**Along $z$.** The outcomes are $+1$ for $|0\rangle$ (spin up) and $-1$ for $|1\rangle$ (spin down):

$$
\sigma_z = (+1)|0\rangle\langle 0| + (-1)|1\rangle\langle 1| = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Along $x$.** The outcomes are $+1$ for $|+\rangle$ and $-1$ for $|-\rangle$, where $|\pm\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)/\sqrt2$:

$$
\sigma_x = |+\rangle\langle +| - |-\rangle\langle -| = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} - \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Along $y$**, using $|\pm i\rangle = (|0\rangle \pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the same recipe gives

$$
\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These are the **Pauli matrices** (Wolfgang Pauli, 1927). The physical spin observables are $\hat S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$, with outcomes $\pm\hbar/2$.

<CSCorner>
You've seen $\sigma_x$ before: it's the **X gate** (NOT), and $\sigma_z$ is the **Z gate**. The *same matrix* plays two completely different roles:

- **As a gate** (a unitary), it's an *action*: apply it and the state changes, $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$.
- **As an observable** (a Hermitian operator), it's a *question*: "is the spin along $+x$ or $-x$?" The matrix encodes the answers and the states that give them.

The Paulis are among the rare matrices that are both unitary *and* Hermitian, because $\sigma_i^2 = I$. Most observables (energy, position) are not unitary, and most gates ($S$, $T$) are not Hermitian.
</CSCorner>

<Warning>
**Multiplying a state by an observable is *not* measuring it.** $\sigma_x|0\rangle = |1\rangle$, but measuring $\sigma_x$ on $|0\rangle$ does *not* give "$|1\rangle$". It gives $+1$ or $-1$ with probability ½ each, and leaves the spin in $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$. The matrix product $\hat A|\psi\rangle$ is a bookkeeping tool (you'll use it to compute averages next lesson), not a physical process.
</Warning>

## Spin along any axis

What about the tilted magnet? A magnet along unit vector $\hat n = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$ measures the component of spin along $\hat n$. Since spin components combine like a vector, the observable is the dot product

$$
\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma \;=\; n_x\sigma_x + n_y\sigma_y + n_z\sigma_z \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} n_z & n_x - i n_y \\ n_x + i n_y & -n_z\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvalues are **always $\pm 1$**. That's the two-spot result, for any axis. The reason is short: the Paulis anticommute ($\sigma_x\sigma_y = -\sigma_y\sigma_x$, and so on) and each squares to $I$. Expand $(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2$ and the cross terms cancel in pairs:

$$
(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = (n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2)\, I = I.
$$

A matrix that squares to $I$ can only have eigenvalues whose squares are $1$, which means $\pm 1$.

So the general eigenvectors are just the Bloch-sphere states pointing along $\pm\hat n$. If $\hat n$ has angles $(\theta,\phi)$:

$$
|{+}n\rangle = \cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle, \qquad |{-}n\rangle = \sin\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle - e^{i\phi}\cos\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle .
$$

<KeyIdea>
For a qubit, **every** two-outcome measurement is "measure spin along some axis $\hat n$". The observable is $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$, the outcomes are $\pm1$, and the outcome states are the two antipodal points $\pm\hat n$. Measuring "in the $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ basis" is the same thing as measuring $\sigma_x$.
</KeyIdea>

## Outcome probabilities from the observable

The observable tells you the outcomes and outcome states. The **Born rule** from World 3 gives the odds:

$$
P(a_k) \;=\; |\langle a_k|\psi\rangle|^2 .
$$

For spin along $\hat n$ there's a lovely geometric shortcut. With $\vec r$ the Bloch vector of $|\psi\rangle$,

$$
P(+1) = \frac{1 + \hat n\cdot\vec r}{2}, \qquad P(-1) = \frac{1 - \hat n\cdot\vec r}{2}.
$$

The probability depends only on the angle between the state and the measurement axis.

  <Hint>The Bloch vector is $(\sin 60°, 0, \cos 60°)$ and $\hat n = (1,0,0)$.</Hint>
  <Explain>$\hat n\cdot\vec r = \sin 60° \approx 0.866$, so $P(+1) = (1 + 0.866)/2 \approx 0.933$.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

<Note title="Degenerate eigenvalues">
Sometimes several orthogonal states share one eigenvalue. Example: the two-qubit **parity** observable $Z\otimes Z$ gives $+1$ for both $|00\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$. Then the outcome corresponds to a whole **eigenspace**, and the lookup table uses a projector onto that subspace: $\hat A = \sum_a a\,P_a$. This turns out to be the key to quantum error correction (World 11): you can measure the parity without learning the individual bits.
</Note>

<History>
In 1925 the 23-year-old **Werner Heisenberg**, recovering from hay fever on the island of Helgoland, worked out a mechanics in which position and momentum were arrays of numbers that didn't commute. **Max Born** recognised the arrays as matrices, and with **Pascual Jordan** the trio built "matrix mechanics" within months. **Pauli** introduced his spin matrices in 1927. In 1932 **John von Neumann** recast the whole theory as linear algebra on Hilbert space, with observables as self-adjoint operators. That is the language you are learning now.
</History>

## Summary

- **Observables are Hermitian operators.** Eigenvalues are the possible outcomes, and eigenvectors are the states in which each outcome is certain.
- Hermitian is forced by physics: real outcomes, and perfectly distinguishable (orthogonal) outcome states.
- Spectral form: $\hat A = \sum_k a_k |a_k\rangle\langle a_k|$, a lookup table from outcomes to states.
- **Pauli matrices** $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ are the spin observables in units of $\hbar/2$. Spin along $\hat n$ is $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$, whose eigenvalues are always $\pm1$ because $(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = I$.
- Its eigenstates are the antipodal Bloch points $\pm\hat n$, and $P(\pm1) = (1 \pm \hat n\cdot\vec r)/2$.
- Applying a matrix to a state is not the same as measuring the observable.
