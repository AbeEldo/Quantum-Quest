## How to read a quantum headline

Four questions sort out most announcements:

1. **What exactly was shown?** Physical qubits or **logical** qubits? *Error-detected* or *error-corrected*? A memory (storing a state), or **logical gates**? A best-case test device, or a full system?
2. **Who checked it?** A **peer-reviewed** paper, a **preprint** (public but unrefereed), or a **press release**? All three can be right, but they deserve different levels of trust.
3. **Compared with what?** Advantage claims are races against the *best known* classical method, and classical methods keep improving. Has the classical-simulation community had time to respond?
4. **Is the task useful?** Random-circuit sampling is a benchmark. Simulating a molecule or a material, or factoring a large number, is a use.

## Error correction: the field's centre of gravity

The biggest story of 2023–2026 is that **error correction stopped being theoretical**.

- **Superconducting:** Google's Willow showed the defining scaling behaviour (Lesson 4): Λ = 2.14, with a logical memory outliving its best physical qubit. Follow-ups in 2025–26 reported color codes, reinforcement-learning-tuned calibration and high-fidelity **magic states** (the ingredient for non-Clifford $T$ gates). IBM is betting on **qLDPC codes** and targets its fault-tolerant **Starling** system (200 logical qubits, $10^8$ gates) for **2029**.
- **Neutral atoms:** Harvard/MIT/QuEra went from 48 error-*detected* logical qubits (2023) to a 448-atom demonstration of the components of a universal fault-tolerant architecture (*Nature*, 2025–26). Atom Computing and Microsoft entangled 24 logical qubits (2024), and other companies have announced similar demos.
- **Trapped ions:** Quantinuum's 98-qubit **Helios** (2025) reported 48 error-corrected logical qubits at a 2:1 physical-to-logical encoding rate, plus larger error-detection experiments.

<Warning>
**"N logical qubits" is not a standard unit.** One group's "logical qubit" may be error-*detected* (errors flagged and the run thrown away), another's fully corrected for many rounds, and another's encoded in a small code with distance 2. Logical error rates per round, the number of correction cycles, and whether *logical gates* were performed matter far more than the headline count.
</Warning>

## Quantum advantage: claims and rebuttals

The pattern since 2019 has been: a striking claim, then a classical counter-attack (better tensor-network contractions, GPU clusters, clever approximations) that shrinks or erases the gap, and then a bigger quantum experiment.

- **2019 Google Sycamore** (random circuits): "10,000 years" was soon cut to days, then hours, by classical simulation.
- **2020– USTC Jiuzhang** (boson sampling) and **Zuchongzhi** (random circuits): similar back-and-forth.
- **2024 Willow** random-circuit sampling: "$10^{25}$ years." No classical match is known, but the task is not useful.
- **2025 D-Wave** (Science): simulation of magnetic-material dynamics on an annealer, partly challenged by classical simulations within weeks.
- **2025 Google "Quantum Echoes"** (Nature): a *verifiable* physics measurement claimed 13,000× faster than the best classical method.
- **2026 IBM** declared that 2026 was the "year of advantage," with results logged on a public **Quantum Advantage Tracker** so classical teams can respond. One July 2026 claim was matched by a 256-GPU computation within weeks, which narrowed the claim but didn't refute it.

The honest summary for late 2026: quantum processors now perform specific, carefully chosen tasks that are at least very hard to simulate classically. **Useful, unambiguous advantage for a practical problem is still being argued about.** Large-scale fault-tolerant applications (chemistry, materials, factoring) remain years away.

<CSCorner>
This is the same dynamic as benchmarks in ML or SAT solving: "beats the best known algorithm" is a moving target. Complexity theory gives *evidence* that tasks like random-circuit sampling are classically hard (World 9's BQP discussion), but no proof, since we can't even prove P ≠ NP. That's why the community increasingly values **verifiable** tasks, where a classical computer can at least check the answer, and **certified** randomness (a 2025 Nature paper by JPMorgan, Quantinuum and collaborators used a trapped-ion machine to generate randomness certified by classical verification).
</CSCorner>

## Cryptography: harvest now, decrypt later

Shor's algorithm (World 9) breaks RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, the public-key systems that protect most of the internet. No quantum computer today comes close. But two facts make this urgent anyway:

- **Harvest now, decrypt later.** An adversary can record encrypted traffic *today* and decrypt it once a large quantum computer exists. Secrets that must stay secret for 10–20 years (medical, diplomatic, industrial) are already at risk.
- **Resource estimates keep falling.** Better algorithms and codes have cut the estimated cost of breaking RSA-2048 by orders of magnitude.

<RsaEstimates />

The response is **post-quantum cryptography (PQC)**: classical algorithms based on math problems (lattices, hashes, codes) that are believed hard even for quantum computers. In **August 2024** NIST published the first three standards: **FIPS 203 (ML-KEM)** for key exchange, and **FIPS 204 (ML-DSA)** and **FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA)** for signatures. In March 2025 it selected **HQC** as a backup key-exchange scheme. Browsers and messaging apps have already begun deploying hybrid post-quantum key exchange, and governments have set migration deadlines in the 2030s.

<Warning>
**"Quantum computers will make all encryption useless."** Shor's algorithm breaks *public-key* schemes built on factoring and discrete logs. Symmetric ciphers like AES are only weakened quadratically by Grover's algorithm (World 9), and doubling key lengths (AES-256) restores the margin. Post-quantum public-key algorithms are designed to resist known quantum attacks. And note the opposite misconception too: **quantum key distribution** (World 8) is a different tool, not a requirement for being "quantum-safe."
</Warning>

## The Majorana controversy: a case study in evidence

In February 2025 Microsoft announced **Majorana 1**, "the world's first quantum processor powered by topological qubits." The peer-reviewed *Nature* paper published the same day made a far narrower claim: a single-shot measurement of a nanowire's fermion **parity**. The journal attached an unusual editorial note that the results "do not represent evidence for the presence of Majorana zero modes." A Microsoft Majorana paper from 2018 had been **retracted** in 2021. In June 2026 *Nature* published a critical Comment arguing that the 2025 data analysis was flawed, along with Microsoft's reply. Microsoft announced a follow-up chip, "Majorana 2," in a preprint the same month.

As of late 2026 there's **no independently verified topological qubit**. That may change, since the physics is sound in principle, but this is a textbook case of why the *paper* and the *press release* must be read separately.

## Summary

- Read headlines with four questions: **what** exactly (physical/logical, detected/corrected, memory/gates), **who checked** (peer review / preprint / press release), **compared to what**, and **is it useful**.
- **QEC is now experimental reality:** below-threshold surface codes (Google, Λ ≈ 2.1), dozens of logical qubits on neutral atoms and ions, and early magic-state and logical-gate work. Fault-tolerant roadmaps target ~2029 and beyond.
- **Advantage claims** keep arriving and keep meeting classical rebuttals. Practical advantage is still debated.
- **Crypto:** resource estimates for RSA-2048 have fallen from ~20 M qubits (2019) to < 1 M (2025), with lower claims under new assumptions. **NIST PQC standards (FIPS 203/204/205, Aug 2024)** are being deployed because of **harvest-now-decrypt-later**.
- **Topological qubits** remain unproven, and the Majorana story is a lesson in weighing evidence.
