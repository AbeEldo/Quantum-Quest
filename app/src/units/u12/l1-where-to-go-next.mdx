<Warning>
**"Now I know quantum computing, so quantum computers will soon do everything faster."** You now know enough to see why that's false. The known exponential speedups are for structured problems (factoring, discrete logs, simulating quantum systems). Grover gives only a square root. Most everyday computing gets no speedup at all, and every useful application needs fault tolerance, which costs hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit.
</Warning>

## Write real code on real qubits

You've been using a simulator written for this course. The same circuits run on industrial software, and several companies let you submit them to **real hardware for free** (small jobs, with queues):

- **Qiskit** (IBM, Python): the most widely used SDK. The IBM Quantum Platform offers free monthly time on real superconducting processors.
- **Cirq** (Google) and **Stim** (a blazing-fast stabilizer simulator, plus **PyMatching** for decoding). If you liked World 11, Stim + PyMatching let you reproduce the threshold plot for millions of shots in minutes.
- **PennyLane** (Xanadu): quantum machine learning and differentiable circuits.
- Cloud access to ions, atoms and superconductors: **Amazon Braket**, **Azure Quantum**, and the vendors' own portals.

<Try>
A good first project: build the Bell-state and teleportation circuits from Worlds 6 and 8 in Qiskit, run them on a real device, and compare the measured histogram with the ideal one. You will *see* World 10's noise in your own data. Then add the 3-qubit repetition code from World 11 and check whether it helps on that hardware.
</Try>

<CSCorner>
Quantum software is a young field with very CS-shaped problems. **Compilers** map circuits to hardware with limited connectivity (routing is NP-hard in general). **Verification** checks that two circuits are equivalent. **Decoders** are real-time, latency-critical graph algorithms. **Resource estimation** works out how many qubits and how much time an algorithm really needs. **Classical simulation** with tensor networks and stabilizer methods is the benchmark every advantage claim must beat. Every one of these is an active research area that welcomes computer scientists.
</CSCorner>

## Read next

**Books and notes**

- **Nielsen & Chuang**, *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information*: the standard reference. Chapters 8–10 deepen Worlds 10–11.
- **John Preskill's Ph219 lecture notes** (Caltech, free online): especially chapter 7 on error correction.
- **Scott Aaronson**, *Quantum Computing Since Democritus*: complexity theory, with jokes. His blog *Shtetl-Optimized* is the best running commentary on advantage claims.
- **N. David Mermin**, *Quantum Computer Science*: a short, elegant book for computer scientists.
- **Quantum Country** (Matuschak & Nielsen): an essay with built-in spaced repetition, the inspiration for this course's flashcards.
- The **Error Correction Zoo** website: every code you've heard of, and hundreds more.

**Courses:** MIT OCW 8.04/8.05 (quantum physics), IBM's Qiskit courses, Umesh Vazirani's quantum computing lectures, and John Watrous's notes on quantum information theory.

**Video:** 3Blue1Brown's quantum computing and Grover videos, and the talks from the yearly **QIP** and **APS March Meeting** conferences (many are free online).

## Where the open problems are

<History>
In 1981, at a conference at MIT, **Richard Feynman** argued that nature isn't classical, "and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical." Forty-plus years later, the field he helped start employs tens of thousands of people. Every major advance in this course, from Shor's algorithm to the threshold theorem to the surface-code experiments, came from someone asking whether a "known" limitation was real.
</History>

A few frontiers where newcomers are making a difference:

- **Better codes:** qLDPC codes that need 10× fewer qubits than the surface code, codes matched to biased noise (cat qubits), and faster logical gates via lattice surgery and magic-state cultivation.
- **Decoding:** real-time decoders on FPGAs and ASICs, neural decoders, and decoders for correlated noise.
- **Algorithms:** useful quantum simulation for chemistry and materials, provable speedups beyond Shor and Grover, and dequantisation (classical algorithms that erase claimed speedups).
- **Complexity:** what can noisy, non-error-corrected devices provably do? Which "advantage" tasks are really hard?
- **Hardware engineering:** materials, fabrication, cryogenics, control electronics and photonic links. Careers here span physics, EE and CS.
- **Cryptography migration:** moving the world's infrastructure to post-quantum cryptography is a decade-long software-engineering project happening *now*.

  <Q>You want to reproduce a surface-code threshold plot with millions of noisy shots, on a laptop, this weekend. Which tools fit best?</Q>
  <Opt>A dense state-vector simulator with 49 qubits</Opt>
  <Opt correct>A stabilizer simulator (e.g. Stim) plus a matching decoder (e.g. PyMatching)</Opt>
  <Opt>A real quantum computer with 49 physical qubits</Opt>
  <Opt>A density-matrix simulator</Opt>
  <Explain>Surface-code circuits are Clifford circuits with Pauli noise, which the Gottesman–Knill theorem says are efficiently simulable classically. A dense 49-qubit state vector would need $2^{49}$ amplitudes (petabytes), and a density matrix is worse.</Explain>
</Quiz>

## Summary

- The thread of the course: **amplitudes interfere** → qubits as **vectors on a sphere** → **entanglement** as a resource → **circuits and algorithms** that choreograph interference → **noise** that measures your qubits for you → **error correction** that fights back by measuring only harmless questions.
- To keep going, run real circuits (**Qiskit, Cirq, PennyLane, Stim**), read **Nielsen & Chuang**, **Preskill**, **Aaronson** and **Mermin**, and follow the **arXiv** and conference talks, with healthy skepticism toward headlines.
- Open problems need computer scientists: codes, decoders, compilers, verification, algorithms, complexity, and post-quantum migration.
