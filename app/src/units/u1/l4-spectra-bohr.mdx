## Barcodes of the elements

<History>
In 1814 Joseph von Fraunhofer, a master lens-maker, looked at sunlight through a fine prism and found it crossed by hundreds of **dark lines**. In 1859–60 Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen (of burner fame) showed that each element, heated in a flame, emits bright lines at its own characteristic wavelengths, and that a cool gas **absorbs** at exactly the same wavelengths it emits. The dark lines in sunlight are the fingerprints of elements in the Sun's atmosphere absorbing light from the hotter surface beneath.

Spectroscopy became the first way to learn what stars are made of. In 1868, observers of a solar eclipse saw a yellow line at 587.6 nm that matched no known element. Norman Lockyer named the mystery element **helium**, after the Greek *helios*, the Sun. It wasn't found on Earth until 1895.
</History>

Physicists now had a precise, reproducible pattern of numbers for each element. But nobody knew *why* atoms emit only certain wavelengths. Classical physics had no explanation at all.

The first breakthrough came from numerology. In 1885 Johann Balmer, a Swiss schoolteacher, noticed that the four visible hydrogen wavelengths fit a simple formula:

$$
\lambda = B\,\frac{n^2}{n^2 - 4}, \qquad B = 364.56\ \text{nm}, \quad n = 3, 4, 5, 6.
$$

In 1888 Johannes Rydberg recast it in the form that turned out to generalize, using inverse wavelengths:

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad R_H \approx 1.0968\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1},\quad n_2 > n_1 .
$$

Balmer's lines are the ones with $n_1 = 2$. The formula predicted other series, and they were found: $n_1 = 1$ in the ultraviolet (Lyman, 1906–1914) and $n_1 = 3$ in the infrared (Paschen, 1908).

## The atom that shouldn't exist

By 1911, Ernest Rutherford's scattering experiments had revealed the structure of the atom: a tiny, heavy, positive **nucleus** with light negative electrons somewhere around it. The obvious picture is a miniature solar system, electrons orbiting the nucleus held by electric attraction.

Classical physics says this atom is a disaster:

- An orbiting electron is constantly accelerating, and Maxwell's equations say **accelerating charges radiate**. The electron should lose energy, spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus. The calculated time for a hydrogen atom is about **$10^{-11}$ seconds**.
- As it spirals in, its orbital frequency changes continuously, so it should emit a **continuous smear** of frequencies, not sharp lines.

Yet atoms are stable for billions of years and emit sharp lines. Something is badly wrong.

## Bohr's quantum atom

In 1913 Niels Bohr, a 27-year-old Dane fresh from a stay in Rutherford's laboratory in Manchester, proposed rules that simply *forbade* the classical disaster. They were not derived from anything; they were bold postulates, justified by their results.

<KeyIdea>
**Bohr's postulates (1913).**

1. The electron can occupy only certain **stationary states** (allowed orbits) with definite energies $E_1, E_2, E_3, \dots$, and while in one it does **not** radiate.
2. Light is emitted or absorbed only when the electron **jumps** between stationary states, as a single photon carrying the energy difference:
$$
hf = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}} .
$$
3. The allowed orbits are those whose angular momentum is a whole multiple of $\hbar = h/2\pi$: $\;L = n\hbar,\ n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$
</KeyIdea>

Combining postulate 3 with Newton's laws and Coulomb's law gives orbit radii $r_n = n^2 a_0$ (with $a_0 = 0.0529$ nm, the **Bohr radius**) and energies

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}.
$$

The energy is negative because the electron is bound: you need to *add* 13.6 eV to free it from the ground state $n=1$. Each jump down emits a photon with $hc/\lambda = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1} = 13.6\ \text{eV}\,(1/n_1^2 - 1/n_2^2)$, which is **exactly Rydberg's formula**, and Bohr's theory predicts the value of $R_H$ from $h$, $e$, $m_e$ and $c$. <DeepDive title="Deriving Bohr's energy levels">
For a circular orbit of radius $r$ and speed $v$, the Coulomb force provides the centripetal force:
$$
\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{k e^2}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.
$$
Bohr's condition: $L = m v r = n\hbar$, so $v = n\hbar/(mr)$. Substituting,
$$
r_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{m k e^2} = n^2 a_0, \qquad a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{m k e^2} \approx 0.0529\ \text{nm}.
$$
The total energy is kinetic plus potential, $E = \tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac{ke^2}{r} = -\tfrac{ke^2}{2r}$ (using the force balance), so
$$
E_n = -\frac{ke^2}{2a_0}\frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{m k^2 e^4}{2\hbar^2}\frac{1}{n^2} \approx -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}.
$$
Every constant is a known fundamental constant: no fitting.
</DeepDive>

## Triumph, and the limits of a stopgap

Bohr's model was a sensation. Beyond hydrogen's lines, it explained the spectrum of ionized helium (one electron around a doubly charged nucleus) and the pattern of X-ray energies across the periodic table. Einstein called it "the highest form of musicality in the sphere of thought". Bohr received the 1922 Nobel Prize.

But it was clearly a patch, not a theory:

- It works only for one-electron atoms. It fails badly for neutral helium, with just two electrons.
- It can't say how *bright* each line is, or how long an atom stays excited.
- It doesn't explain *why* angular momentum should be quantized, or why a stationary orbit doesn't radiate. It just declares it.
- High-resolution spectra show that lines split into close pairs and triplets (fine structure), especially in a magnetic field, beyond the model's reach.

The real answer came in 1925–26 with quantum mechanics proper (Heisenberg, Schrödinger and others), which reproduces Bohr's energy levels exactly while abandoning orbits altogether.

<Warning>
"Electrons orbit the nucleus like tiny planets." This is Bohr's 1913 picture, and it is wrong. In modern quantum mechanics an electron in an atom has no trajectory at all. It's described by a **wavefunction** (an "orbital") spread out around the nucleus, and the allowed energies come from the requirement that this wave fits around the nucleus consistently. Bohr's energies survive, but his orbits don't. The atom icon with elliptical orbits is a logo, not a picture.
</Warning>

<CSCorner>
Spectral lines make atoms the most reproducible oscillators in the universe, and that's why **time** itself is now defined by one. Since 1967 the second has been defined as exactly 9 192 631 770 periods of the radiation from a particular transition in caesium-133 (between two hyperfine levels of its ground state). Every GPS satellite carries atomic clocks; your phone's position is computed from timing differences of nanoseconds. A quantum jump is the master clock behind the internet's time synchronization.
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- Glowing gases emit (and cool gases absorb) light only at sharp, element-specific wavelengths: **line spectra**.
- Hydrogen fits Rydberg's formula $1/\lambda = R_H(1/n_1^2 - 1/n_2^2)$; the visible **Balmer** lines (656, 486, 434, 410 nm) all end at $n_1 = 2$.
- Classical atoms would collapse in about $10^{-11}$ s and emit continuous spectra.
- **Bohr (1913):** stationary states with $L = n\hbar$, energies $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$; photons carry the energy difference $hf = E_u - E_l$. This reproduces Rydberg's formula from fundamental constants.
- The model fails beyond hydrogen-like atoms; real quantum mechanics keeps the energy levels but replaces orbits with wavefunctions.
