By the end of the 19th century, physicists were confident they understood light. It was a **wave**: a ripple in the electromagnetic field, travelling at $3\times10^8$ m/s. That picture had beaten Newton's rival "particles of light" in a series of decisive experiments, and Maxwell's equations explained it all with breathtaking economy.

This world is the story of how that confidence collapsed. To feel the shock properly, you first need to know exactly what the wave picture predicts, and why everyone believed it. So we start in the bathtub.

## What a wave is

A wave is a pattern that travels while the medium itself only wiggles in place. A steady wave travelling along $x$ looks like

$$
y(x,t) = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \varphi),
$$

with these parts:

- **amplitude** $A$: how big the wiggle is;
- **wavelength** $\lambda = 2\pi/k$: the distance from crest to crest;
- **frequency** $f = \omega/2\pi$: how many crests pass per second (unit: hertz, Hz). The **period** is $T = 1/f$;
- **phase** $\varphi$: where in its cycle the wave starts.

In one period, a crest moves forward one wavelength, so the wave speed is

$$
v = f\lambda .
$$

Some numbers worth carrying around:

| Wave | Speed | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound in air | 343 m/s | concert A, 440 Hz: $\lambda = 343/440 \approx 0.78$ m |
| Water ripples | ~0.2–0.3 m/s | $\lambda \sim 1$–$2$ cm in a ripple tank |
| Light (vacuum) | $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s | green light, $\lambda = 550$ nm: $f \approx 5.45\times10^{14}$ Hz |

Visible light spans wavelengths from about **380 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red)**. Beyond violet is ultraviolet (UV); beyond red, infrared (IR). Remember that ordering. It is about to matter a great deal.

## Superposition in a ripple tank

When two waves overlap, the displacement at each point is the sum of the two displacements. For waves of the same frequency, that's the phasor sum from World 0: each source contributes a little rotating arrow at each point, and its angle depends on how far the wave has travelled.

<KeyIdea>
**Intensity goes as amplitude squared.** The energy carried by a wave is proportional to $A^2$, not $A$. Two equal waves meeting in step have twice the amplitude and **four** times the intensity; out of step, zero. Averaged over the whole pattern, energy is conserved: the bright bands get exactly what the dark bands lose.
</KeyIdea>

## The geometry of fringes

Look at a distant point at angle $\theta$ from the centre line. The wave from the far source travels an extra distance $d\sin\theta$ to get there. From World 0: whole wavelengths of path difference give constructive interference, and half-odd wavelengths destructive. So the bright directions satisfy

$$
d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots
$$

On a screen at distance $L$ (much bigger than $d$), small angles give $\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta = y/L$, and the bright fringes sit at heights

$$
y_m = \frac{m\lambda L}{d}, \qquad \text{spacing } \Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}.
$$

This formula is a **ruler for wavelengths**. Measure the fringe spacing $\Delta y$, the slit separation $d$ and the distance $L$, and you can compute a wavelength far too small to see directly.

## How light became a wave

<History>
In the 1700s most physicists followed Isaac Newton, who thought light was a stream of tiny particles ("corpuscles"). Christiaan Huygens had argued for waves, but Newton's authority won.

Then, between 1801 and 1803, **Thomas Young** split a thin beam of sunlight using the edge of a card and saw coloured interference fringes, exactly the pattern you just made in the ripple tank, with fringe spacings that let him estimate the wavelengths of the colours (around half a micron). His famous two-slit version appears in his 1807 *Lectures*. In 1818 **Augustin-Jean Fresnel** submitted a full wave theory of diffraction to the French Academy. Siméon Poisson, a judge, objected that it predicted an absurdity: a bright spot at the centre of the shadow of a disc. **François Arago** looked, and found the spot.

In 1865 **James Clerk Maxwell** showed that electric and magnetic fields obey a wave equation with speed $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, numerically equal to the measured speed of light. Light was an electromagnetic wave. In 1887 **Heinrich Hertz** generated and detected such waves (radio) in his laboratory.
</History>

By 1900 the wave theory looked unassailable. And it made a very specific claim about **energy**: a light wave carries energy proportional to the square of its field amplitude, spread continuously over the wavefront. A brighter light means a bigger amplitude. Frequency (colour) has nothing to do with how much energy arrives; you can deliver as much energy as you like at any colour by turning up the brightness.

Hold onto that claim. In the next three lessons it fails three different experiments.

<Warning>
"Doubling the amplitude doubles the brightness." No: brightness (intensity, power per area) goes as amplitude **squared**, so doubling the amplitude gives four times the intensity. This square law is the same one that makes probabilities in quantum mechanics the *square* of amplitudes, and it's why interference can create dark spots: amplitudes can cancel, but squares are never negative.
</Warning>

## Three cracks in the picture

Around 1900, three experimental puzzles refused to fit:

1. **Blackbody radiation.** Hot objects glow with a characteristic spectrum. Classical physics predicted they should radiate *infinite* energy at short wavelengths: the "ultraviolet catastrophe". (Lesson 2.)
2. **The photoelectric effect.** Light knocks electrons out of metals, but whether it can do so depends on its **colour**, not its brightness. (Lesson 3.)
3. **Atomic spectra.** Glowing gases emit light only at a few sharp wavelengths, like a barcode. Classical atoms should glow at all wavelengths, and should not even be stable. (Lesson 4.)

Each crack was eventually explained by the same strange idea: energy in light and matter comes in **discrete lumps**, quanta. And in a final twist (lesson 5), it turns out that particles like electrons also *interfere like waves*.

<CSCorner>
The ripple-tank simulation is pure linear algebra. Because the wave equation is **linear**, the total field is a sum of the fields from each source, and for a single frequency each source's field is a complex phasor. So the whole picture is "compute $\sum_s e^{ikr_s}/\sqrt{r_s}$ at every pixel once, then multiply by $e^{-i\omega t}$ each frame". That's embarrassingly parallel (one independent calculation per pixel), the kind of workload GPUs were built for. Quantum mechanics is linear in exactly the same way, which is what makes superposition and interference possible there too.
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- A wave $A\cos(kx-\omega t+\varphi)$ has amplitude, wavelength $\lambda$, frequency $f$, phase, and speed $v = f\lambda$. Visible light: 380–750 nm, with $f\sim5\times10^{14}$ Hz.
- **Superposition:** overlapping waves add. For one frequency, that means adding phasors at each point.
- **Intensity ∝ amplitude².** Interference redistributes energy into bright and dark fringes.
- Two sources: bright where $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$; on a distant screen, fringe spacing $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$.
- Young (1801–1807), Fresnel and Arago (1818), Maxwell (1865) and Hertz (1887) made light an electromagnetic wave whose energy depends on intensity, not colour.
- Three experiments (blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect and atomic spectra) were about to break that picture.
