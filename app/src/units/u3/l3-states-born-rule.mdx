The Stern–Gerlach experiments gave us a list of hard facts:

1. Along any axis, exactly **two** outcomes.
2. Repeat the same measurement and you get the **same** answer.
3. Prepare $z+$, measure $x$: **50/50**. Prepare $x+$, measure $z$: also **50/50**.
4. Prepare $z+$, measure along an axis tilted by $\theta$: "+" with probability $\cos^2(\theta/2)$.

Now we build the mathematical model that reproduces *all* of them. It's the same model, line for line, that runs every quantum computer, so this lesson is where "spin" officially becomes "**qubit**".

---

## States are unit vectors

<Definition term="Qubit state">
The state of a spin-½ particle (or any two-level system) is a **unit vector** in $\mathbb{C}^2$. Name the "up along $z$" and "down along $z$" states
$$
|\!\uparrow\rangle \equiv |0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\!\downarrow\rangle \equiv |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
They form an **orthonormal basis**, so every state can be written as a **superposition**
$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 .
$$
</Definition>

$|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are **orthogonal**, $\langle 0|1\rangle = 0$, because they are the two mutually exclusive answers to the same question: an atom that is definitely $z+$ is *never* found $z-$.

And here is the rule connecting vectors to experiments:

<KeyIdea title="The Born rule">
A measurement has an orthonormal basis of possible outcomes $\{|b_0\rangle, |b_1\rangle\}$. For a system in state $|\psi\rangle$:
$$
P(b_k) = |\langle b_k|\psi\rangle|^2 ,
$$
and immediately afterwards the system is in the state $|b_k\rangle$ (**collapse**).
</KeyIdea>

For the $z$ measurement this just says $P(0) = |\alpha|^2$ and $P(1) = |\beta|^2$. Collapse explains fact 2: once you're in $|0\rangle$, measuring $z$ again gives $|\langle 0|0\rangle|^2 = 1$.

<NumQuiz id="u3l3-p1" answer={0.25} tol={0.005}>
  <Q>A spin is in the state $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac12|1\rangle$. What's the probability of finding it **spin-down** along $z$?</Q>
  <Explain>$P(1) = |\langle 1|\psi\rangle|^2 = (1/2)^2 = 1/4$. And $P(0) = 3/4$; they sum to 1 because the vector is normalized.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

## Finding the $x$ states

Which vector is $|{+x}\rangle$, "up along $x$"? Fact 3 says $|\langle 0|{+x}\rangle|^2 = |\langle 1|{+x}\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$, so both components have magnitude $1/\sqrt2$. The simplest choice is

$$
|+\rangle \equiv |{+x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big), \qquad |-\rangle \equiv |{-x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\big).
$$

Check: they're orthogonal ($\langle +|-\rangle = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$), each is 50/50 in the $z$ basis, and $|0\rangle$ is 50/50 in the $x$ basis: $|\langle\pm|0\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$. ✓ That's the whole of Experiment 3: $z+ \xrightarrow{\text{SG-}x} |+\rangle \xrightarrow{\text{SG-}z}$ 50/50.

For real amplitudes you can **see** this. Draw states as arrows in a plane: $|0\rangle$ points right, $|1\rangle$ points up, and $|\pm\rangle$ point along the diagonals. A measurement basis is a pair of perpendicular axes, and each probability is the **squared length of the projection** onto an axis.

## Tilted analyzers and the half-angle

For an analyzer tilted by $\theta$ from $z$ toward $x$, the "up" state is

$$
|{+\hat n}\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad |{-\hat n}\rangle = -\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle .
$$

The Born rule then gives $P(+\hat n \mid 0) = |\langle{+\hat n}|0\rangle|^2 = \cos^2(\theta/2)$, exactly the measured fact 4. Notice what happened. A **physical** rotation of the magnet by $\theta$ corresponds to rotating the **state vector** by only $\theta/2$ in the plane. Spin-up and spin-down point in *opposite* directions in space ($180°$), but their state vectors are perpendicular ($90°$). The little dial in the widget shows the physical spin direction, at twice the vector's angle.

## Why complex numbers are unavoidable

There's a third axis, $y$. By symmetry, $|{\pm y}\rangle$ must be 50/50 when measured along $z$ **and** along $x$. Try to find such a vector with *real* amplitudes. In the plane picture you need an arrow at $45°$ to both the $z$ axes and the $x$ axes, and there isn't room: the diagonals are already taken by $|\pm\rangle$.

With complex amplitudes there is room:

$$
|{+i}\rangle \equiv |{+y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle\big), \qquad |{-i}\rangle \equiv |{-y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle - i|1\rangle\big).
$$

Check against $x$ (remember the bra conjugates): $\langle +|{+i}\rangle = \tfrac12(1 + i)$, and $|1+i|^2/4 = 2/4 = \tfrac12$. ✓

<NumQuiz id="u3l3-plus-y" answer={0.5} tol={0.005}>
  <Q>A spin is prepared in $|{+i}\rangle$. It's measured along $x$. What is $P(x-)$?</Q>
  <Hint>$|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$, so $\langle -|{+i}\rangle = \tfrac12(1 - i)$.</Hint>
  <Explain>$|1 - i|^2/4 = 2/4 = 1/2$. Spin up along $y$ is 50/50 along both $x$ and $z$: three mutually "unbiased" bases.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

So three perpendicular directions in space require **complex** two-dimensional vectors. The Stern–Gerlach facts alone force amplitudes to be complex. Here's the full dictionary:

| Spin "up along…" | Ket | Amplitudes | Photon polarization |
|---|---|---|---|
| $+z$ | $\lvert 0\rangle$ | $(1, 0)$ | $H$ (horizontal) |
| $-z$ | $\lvert 1\rangle$ | $(0, 1)$ | $V$ (vertical) |
| $+x$ | $\lvert +\rangle$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ | $D$ (diagonal, $45°$) |
| $-x$ | $\lvert -\rangle$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ | $A$ (anti-diagonal, $135°$) |
| $+y$ | $\lvert {+i}\rangle$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, i)$ | circular |
| $-y$ | $\lvert {-i}\rangle$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -i)$ | the other circular |

## One qubit, many costumes

The last column shows that a photon's polarization is **the same mathematical object** as an electron's spin. Both are unit vectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$ obeying the Born rule. The physical *angles* differ by a factor of 2: a polarizer at angle $\alpha$ corresponds to a spin analyzer at angle $2\alpha$. That's why orthogonal polarizations ($H$, $V$) are $90°$ apart while orthogonal spins ($z+$, $z-$) are $180°$ apart. Flip the **show as photon polarization** toggle in either widget above to see the same vector relabelled.

This is the deep reason quantum computing doesn't care what a qubit is made of. Trapped-ion qubits use two energy levels of an ion, superconducting "transmon" qubits use the two lowest energy levels of a nonlinear circuit, photonic qubits use polarization or paths, and spin qubits use electron or nuclear spins. **Any two-level quantum system is a qubit**, with the same math every time.

  <Q>Alice hands you a spin. Either it's in $|+\rangle$, or she flipped a fair coin and prepared $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ accordingly (without telling you which). Is there any single measurement that tells these two situations apart better than guessing?</Q>
  <Opt>No: both give 50/50 when you measure along $z$, so they're the same thing</Opt>
  <Opt correct>Yes: measure along $x$. $|+\rangle$ always gives $x+$; the coin-flip spin gives $x+$ only half the time</Opt>
  <Opt>Only by measuring infinitely many times</Opt>
  <Explain>Not with certainty from one copy, but a single $x$ measurement is already informative. If you see $x-$, it was definitely the coin-flip spin. Over a handful of copies the difference becomes overwhelming. A superposition is **not** "secretly 0 or 1, we just don't know which". It's a definite state, pointing along $+x$.</Explain>
</Predict>

<Warning>
"A qubit in $|+\rangle$ is half 0 and half 1" or "it's really 0 or 1 and we don't know which". Both are wrong. $|+\rangle$ is a perfectly definite state: spin up along $x$, which gives a **certain** answer to the $x$ question. It only looks random if you insist on asking the $z$ question. "Superposition" is always relative to a chosen basis.
</Warning>

<CSCorner>
Compare a **probabilistic bit** with a **qubit**:

| | probabilistic bit | qubit |
|---|---|---|
| state | $(p_0, p_1)$, $p_i \ge 0$ | $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{C}^2$ |
| normalization | $p_0 + p_1 = 1$ ($L^1$ norm) | $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2 + \lvert\beta\rvert^2 = 1$ ($L^2$ norm) |
| readout | see $i$ with prob. $p_i$ | see $i$ with prob. $\lvert\text{amp}_i\rvert^2$ |
| other bases? | no | yes, infinitely many |

The switch from the 1-norm to the 2-norm, with complex entries, is the entire difference, and it's what allows cancellation (World 2). Scott Aaronson likes to describe quantum mechanics as "probability theory with the 2-norm instead of the 1-norm".
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- A qubit (spin-½) state is a unit vector $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$; $|0\rangle = |\!\uparrow\rangle$, $|1\rangle = |\!\downarrow\rangle$.
- **Born rule:** measuring in an orthonormal basis $\{|b_k\rangle\}$ gives outcome $k$ with probability $|\langle b_k|\psi\rangle|^2$ and leaves the state in $|b_k\rangle$.
- Measurement bases: $z$: $|0\rangle, |1\rangle$; $x$: $|\pm\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle)$; $y$: $|{\pm i}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle \pm i|1\rangle)$.
- A tilted analyzer at physical angle $\theta$ has up state $\cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle + \sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle$: **state angles are half of physical angles.**
- The $y$ axis forces amplitudes to be **complex**.
- Photon polarization, spin, ion levels, transmons: all the same qubit, with polarization angles doubled to match spin angles.
