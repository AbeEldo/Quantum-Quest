Quantum hardware companies love to quote **gate fidelities**: "99.9% single-qubit fidelity!", "99.5% two-qubit fidelity!" Those sound almost perfect. A 99.5% exam score is an A+.

But a quantum computation isn't one gate. It's thousands, or eventually billions, and every one of them must work.

To reason about this properly we need a precise language for "a gate that mostly works." That language is **quantum channels**.

## The most general thing that can happen to a qubit

Unitaries describe *closed* systems. A real qubit is never closed: it's always coupled to the wires, the chip and stray fields. The right model is

1. bring in an environment $E$ (in some state $|e_0\rangle$),
2. let system and environment evolve together under a joint unitary $U$,
3. stop looking at the environment, which means taking the partial trace from Lesson 1.

Working this out (see the deep dive) always gives a map of the same form:

<Definition term="Quantum channel (Kraus form)">
$$
\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_k K_k\,\rho\,K_k^\dagger, \qquad \sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = I .
$$

The matrices $K_k$ are **Kraus operators**. The condition $\sum K^\dagger K = I$ guarantees the trace stays 1. Any such map sends density matrices to density matrices, *even when applied to half of an entangled pair*. Mathematicians call these maps **completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP)**.
</Definition>

Read the Kraus form as a story: "with some probability, operation $K_k$ happened to the qubit, and the environment knows which one, but you don't." A unitary gate is the special case with a single Kraus operator $K_0 = U$.

<DeepDive title="Where Kraus operators come from (Stinespring)">
Write the joint evolution as $U(|\psi\rangle|e_0\rangle)$ and expand the environment in a basis $\{|e_k\rangle\}$:

$$
U\,(|\psi\rangle \otimes |e_0\rangle) = \sum_k (K_k|\psi\rangle)\otimes|e_k\rangle, \qquad K_k = \langle e_k|U|e_0\rangle .
$$

Tracing out the environment gives $\rho_S = \sum_k K_k|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|K_k^\dagger$, and unitarity of $U$ gives $\sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = I$.

**Example: the bit-flip channel.** Let the environment be one qubit in $\sqrt{1-p}\,|0\rangle + \sqrt p\,|1\rangle$, and let it act as the control of a CNOT on our qubit. Then $K_0 = \sqrt{1-p}\,I$ and $K_1 = \sqrt p\,X$. So "an X happens with probability p" is literally "our qubit got entangled with a stray qubit that we then lost track of." The converse also holds: every channel arises this way (Stinespring's dilation theorem).
</DeepDive>

## A zoo of noise

Five channels cover most of what you'll meet in papers and datasheets:

| Channel | Kraus operators | What it does to the Bloch ball |
|---|---|---|
| Bit flip | $\sqrt{1-p}\,I,\ \sqrt p\,X$ | shrinks $y, z$ by $(1-2p)$; keeps $x$ |
| Phase flip | $\sqrt{1-p}\,I,\ \sqrt p\,Z$ | shrinks $x, y$ by $(1-2p)$; keeps $z$ |
| Depolarizing | $\sqrt{1-\tfrac{3p}{4}}\,I,\ \tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}X,\ \tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}Y,\ \tfrac{\sqrt p}{2}Z$ | shrinks everything by $(1-p)$: $\rho \to (1-p)\rho + p\,\tfrac{I}{2}$ |
| Amplitude damping ($T_1$) | $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt\gamma\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ | shrinks $x,y$ by $\sqrt{1-\gamma}$ and pushes toward $\lvert 0\rangle$ |
| Phase damping ($T_2$) | $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\sqrt{1-\lambda}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\sqrt\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ | shrinks $x,y$ by $\sqrt{1-\lambda}$; keeps $z$ |

Every single-qubit channel acts on the Bloch ball as an **affine map**, $\vec r \mapsto A\vec r + \vec b$. It squashes the sphere into an ellipsoid, and possibly shifts it. Watch:

<Try>
- **Phase flip** with $p = 0.5$ flattens the sphere into a *line segment*: the $z$ axis. Every coherence is gone, every population survives. This is "complete dephasing."
- **Depolarizing**: crank "apply n times" up. The ball shrinks geometrically toward the centre, $(1-p)^n$.
- **Amplitude damping** is the only channel here whose ellipsoid *moves*. Applied many times, the whole ball collapses onto the north pole, because $|0\rangle$ is its fixed point.
- Compare phase flip and phase damping. They're the same channel in disguise: phase damping with $\lambda$ equals phase flip with $p = \tfrac12(1 - \sqrt{1-\lambda})$.
</Try>

## Measuring damage: fidelity

How close is the noisy output $\rho$ to the state $|\psi\rangle$ we wanted? For a pure target, the standard figure of merit is the **fidelity**

$$
F(|\psi\rangle, \rho) = \langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle = \tfrac12\left(1 + \vec r_\psi \cdot \vec r_\rho\right) \quad\text{(for one qubit)},
$$

the probability that a measurement asking "are you $|\psi\rangle$?" says yes. $F = 1$ means perfect. A random guess on one qubit gives $\tfrac12$. The **gate fidelity** quoted by hardware groups is this quantity averaged over all input states. The **error rate** is roughly $1 - F$, measured with techniques like randomized benchmarking.

<NumQuiz id="u10l3-depolF" answer={0.95} tol={0.002}>
  <Q>A depolarizing channel with $p = 0.1$ acts on $|0\rangle$. What is the fidelity of the output with $|0\rangle$?</Q>
  <Hint>$\rho' = (1-p)|0\rangle\langle 0| + p\,I/2$. Compute $\langle 0|\rho'|0\rangle$.</Hint>
  <Explain>$(1-p)\cdot 1 + p\cdot\tfrac12 = 1 - p/2 = 0.95$. Half the time a "replaced by noise" qubit happens to look right anyway.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

<CSCorner>
The **bit-flip channel is Shannon's binary symmetric channel**, the textbook model of a noisy wire. A classical channel is a stochastic matrix acting on probability vectors, and a quantum channel is its generalisation acting on density matrices. On diagonal (classical) inputs, a quantum channel reduces to a stochastic matrix. The new feature is what happens to the off-diagonal coherences: phase-flip noise has **no classical analogue at all**, since it is invisible to a $Z$ measurement and yet destroys interference. That's one reason quantum error correction has to be cleverer than classical coding (World 11).
</CSCorner>

## Errors compound: the Noisy Circuit Lab

Time to see the opening puzzle play out on a real simulation. The lab below runs circuits on a **density-matrix simulator**: after every gate it applies a depolarizing channel, and after every time step it applies amplitude and phase damping to every qubit.

Two experiments are built in:

- **Mirror circuits.** Run $k$ random layers $U$ and then their exact inverse $U^\dagger$. Ideally you return to $|0\cdots 0\rangle$ every time, so any deviation is pure noise. Hardware groups really do benchmark processors this way.
- **GHZ memory.** Prepare $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\cdots0\rangle + |1\cdots1\rangle)$ and simply *wait*.

## Why deep circuits fail

A useful rule of thumb: if gate $i$ has error rate $\varepsilon_i$, the probability that a run is error-free is about

$$
F_{\text{circuit}} \approx \prod_i (1-\varepsilon_i) \approx e^{-\sum_i \varepsilon_i}.
$$

So **the total error budget $\sum\varepsilon_i$ must stay below about 1**. Some numbers:

| Circuit | Gates | Error per gate | $e^{-\sum\varepsilon}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small demo | 100 | 0.5% | ≈ 0.61 |
| 100 qubits × depth 100 | 10,000 | 0.1% | ≈ 5×10⁻⁵ |
| 100 qubits × depth 100 | 10,000 | 0.01% | ≈ 0.37 |
| Factoring RSA-2048 (Shor) | billions | anything physical | ≈ 0 |

No conceivable improvement in materials gets physical error rates to $10^{-10}$. Something qualitatively different is needed.

<Warning>
**"More qubits means a more powerful quantum computer."** Only if they're good enough. Adding qubits adds gates and therefore errors. A 1,000-qubit chip whose circuits decohere after 50 layers can't run anything a 50-layer circuit on fewer qubits couldn't. That is why the field increasingly reports *logical* qubits, error rates and "quantum volume"-style metrics, not raw qubit counts.
</Warning>

<History>
In 2018 **John Preskill** coined the term **NISQ**, Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum, for the era of 50–1,000-qubit machines without error correction. He was candid: they might find uses, but "noise will limit the size of quantum circuits that can be executed reliably." Since then, **error mitigation** techniques (zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation) have squeezed more out of noisy devices by running many noisy variants and extrapolating, at a sampling cost that grows exponentially with circuit size. The long-term answer is **quantum error correction**, the subject of World 11.
</History>

## Summary

- A **quantum channel** is the most general physical process: $\mathcal E(\rho) = \sum_k K_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ with $\sum K_k^\dagger K_k = I$. It comes from a unitary on system + environment followed by tracing out the environment.
- The standard noise zoo: **bit flip, phase flip, depolarizing, amplitude damping ($T_1$), phase damping ($T_2$)**. Each squashes and possibly shifts the Bloch ball.
- **Fidelity** $F = \langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle$ measures how close the output is to the target. A depolarizing channel gives $F = 1 - p/2$.
- Errors compound: $F_{\text{circuit}}\approx e^{-\sum\varepsilon_i}$. Mirror-circuit and GHZ experiments show fidelity decaying with depth, and entangled states decaying faster with size.
- Useful algorithms need far more gates than physical error rates allow. Mitigation helps a little. **Error correction** is the way out.
