A superconducting qubit sits on a chip inside a dilution refrigerator at about 10 millikelvin, colder than deep space. It's shielded from magnetic fields, light and vibration. You prepare it in $|+\rangle$, do **nothing at all**, and measure along $x$ a fraction of a millisecond later.

Do this a few thousand times and the outcome $|+\rangle$, which should be certain, drifts toward a coin toss. Leave the qubit in $|1\rangle$ instead and, after long enough, it is found in $|0\rangle$. The qubit is **forgetting**. Last lesson's language lets us say precisely how: its state slides off the surface of the Bloch sphere and into the ball.

## Two ways to forget

Experimenters summarise a qubit's forgetfulness with two numbers.

**$T_1$, energy relaxation.** The excited state leaks its energy into the environment: a microwave photon escapes into the wiring, or a defect in the chip absorbs a quantum of vibration. The population of $|1\rangle$ decays exponentially,

$$
P_1(t) = P_1(0)\, e^{-t/T_1} .
$$

On the Bloch ball the vector is dragged up toward the north pole. In the language of the next lesson, this is the **amplitude-damping channel**.

**$T_2$, dephasing.** Even if no energy is exchanged, the *phase* between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ gets scrambled. The coherences decay,

$$
|\rho_{01}(t)| = |\rho_{01}(0)|\, e^{-t/T_2},
$$

so the horizontal part of the Bloch vector shrinks toward the $z$ axis. The populations don't change, but the ability to interfere does.

Energy relaxation also kills coherence: once $|1\rangle$ has decayed, there's no superposition left. Careful accounting gives

$$
\frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\phi},
$$

where $T_\phi$ is the time scale of *pure* dephasing. So **$T_2 \le 2T_1$** always, with equality when nothing but relaxation is going on.

## The density-matrix picture

Combine both effects for a qubit at rest (no driving, in the rotating frame):

$$
\rho(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \rho_{11}(0)\,e^{-t/T_1} & \rho_{01}(0)\, e^{-t/T_2}\,e^{-i\Delta t} \\ \rho_{10}(0)\,e^{-t/T_2}\,e^{+i\Delta t} & \rho_{11}(0)\, e^{-t/T_1} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

**$T_1$ lives on the diagonal and $T_2$ lives off it.** These are the **Bloch equations** that Felix Bloch wrote down in 1946 for nuclear spins in NMR. The same two numbers now appear on the datasheet of every quantum processor.

But *why* would the phase scramble without any energy changing hands? Here is the key picture for the rest of this lesson. The qubit's frequency is never perfectly fixed. A stray magnetic field, a two-level defect flipping in the chip, or a little charge noise shifts the frequency by an amount $\delta$ that you don't know and that varies from run to run. During a wait of length $t$ the qubit picks up an extra phase $\delta t$. Average over runs (or over many spins in a sample) and the arrows **fan out** around the equator. Their average, the signal you measure, shrinks.

<Warning>
**"Dephasing means the qubit is being kicked around randomly at every instant."** Not necessarily. Much of the observed dephasing comes from frequency offsets that are **constant during a single run** but differ between runs, or between spins in a sample. That kind of dephasing is *reversible*. The next two sections show how to undo it. Only noise that fluctuates *during* the evolution is truly irreversible.
</Warning>

## Ramsey: measuring how fast phase is lost

How do you even *see* a phase? You can't measure it directly, but you can turn it into a population. That's the trick of **Ramsey interferometry**:

1. A $\pi/2$ pulse tips the qubit from $|0\rangle$ onto the equator.
2. **Wait** a time $t$. The qubit precesses by an angle $(\Delta_0 + \delta)\,t$, where $\Delta_0$ is the deliberate detuning between the qubit and your reference clock.
3. A second $\pi/2$ pulse rotates the equator back onto the poles, so the accumulated phase becomes a probability: $P(0) = \tfrac12\left[1 + \cos(\Delta_0 t)\right]$ for a perfect qubit.

Sweep the wait time and you get **Ramsey fringes**. With an unknown spread of frequencies, the fringes die off within an envelope whose decay time is called $T_2^*$ ("T-two-star"). That's the dephasing time *including* the reversible part.

<History>
**Norman Ramsey** invented the separated-oscillatory-fields method in 1949 to measure molecular beams more precisely. The same idea runs every caesium atomic clock, which defines the second, and Ramsey shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. Every superconducting or trapped-ion lab runs "Ramsey" sequences daily to calibrate qubit frequencies and measure $T_2^*$.
</History>

## The spin echo: un-scrambling an egg

In 1950 **Erwin Hahn**, a young physicist at the University of Illinois, was doing pulsed NMR on protons. He applied two pulses separated by a time $\tau$ and saw something that seemed impossible: long after the signal had decayed to nothing, it **came back** on its own, a sharp "echo" at time $2\tau$.

Here's the picture. Think of the spins as runners on a track. At the gun ($\pi/2$ pulse) they all start together, but each runs at its own speed, so the pack spreads out. At time $\tau$ everyone is told to **turn around** and run back at the same speed. The fast runners got furthest, so they have the furthest to go back, and they cover it at the same rate. Everyone reaches the start line at the same instant, $2\tau$.

On the Bloch sphere, "turn around" is a **$\pi$ pulse about the $x$ axis**. It reflects each spin's phase $\phi \to -\phi$. A spin with offset $\delta$ has phase $+\delta\tau$ before the pulse, $-\delta\tau$ right after, and $-\delta\tau + \delta\tau = 0$ at $2\tau$, **whatever $\delta$ was**.

<CSCorner>
Think of the echo as a **filter**. Noise that stays constant over one run (frequency close to zero) is cancelled exactly, because the reflection makes the phase picked up before and after the pulse subtract. Noise that varies faster than $1/\tau$ isn't. Adding more π pulses, evenly spaced, makes the high-pass filter steeper. These sequences are called **dynamical decoupling** (CPMG, XY4, UDD, …), and they are the quantum cousin of a signal-processing notch filter, designed in the time domain. The catch: every pulse is itself slightly imperfect, so more pulses aren't always better. You'll feel that tradeoff in the *Coherence Keeper* game.
</CSCorner>

## Real numbers: how long do real qubits remember?

What matters for computing is not the coherence time alone but how many operations fit inside it: **coherence time ÷ gate time**.

| Platform | $T_1$ | $T_2$ (with echo / decoupling) | Two-qubit gate time | Rough ops per coherence time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superconducting transmons | ~50–300 µs in large processors (Google Willow: mean 68–98 µs); lab record ≈ 1.6 ms | ~similar, up to ~2·T₁ | ~20–100 ns | ~10³–10⁴ |
| Trapped ions (hyperfine qubits) | effectively unlimited (no spontaneous decay of the qubit levels) | ~1 s typical; > 1 hour (extrapolated) with heavy decoupling on a single ion | ~10–300 µs | ~10⁴–10⁵ |
| Neutral atoms (hyperfine qubits in tweezers) | seconds (limited by trap light scattering) | ~1 s typical; 12.6 s record in a 6,100-atom array (Caltech, 2025) | ~0.2–1 µs (Rydberg gates), but atom moves take ~ms | ~10³–10⁵ |

<Note title="Approximate and dated">
These are ballpark values as of late 2026, compiled from Google's Willow spec sheet (Dec 2024), Bland et al. (Nature, 2025: 2D transmons with T₁ up to ≈1.68 ms), Wang et al. (Nature Communications, 2021: a ¹⁷¹Yb⁺ ion with an estimated coherence time of about 5,500 s), and Manetsch et al. (Nature, 2025: 6,100 atomic qubits, T₂ = 12.6 s). "Best lab result" and "typical in a 100-qubit processor" can differ by an order of magnitude. Hardware moves fast, so treat any single number with care.
</Note>

Ions and atoms remember far longer than superconducting circuits, but their gates are slower, so the *ratio* is closer than the raw numbers suggest. In every platform, though, the ratio is at most about $10^5$, while useful algorithms like Shor's need billions of reliable operations. Echoes and better materials buy factors of a few. To bridge the remaining gap we will need **error correction** (World 11).

## Summary

- **$T_1$ (relaxation):** populations decay toward the ground state, $P_1 \propto e^{-t/T_1}$. The Bloch vector is pulled to the north pole.
- **$T_2$ (dephasing):** coherences decay, $|\rho_{01}| \propto e^{-t/T_2}$. The Bloch vector shrinks toward the $z$ axis. Always $T_2 \le 2T_1$.
- **$T_1$ lives on the diagonal of ρ and $T_2$ off it** (the Bloch equations).
- **Ramsey** ($\pi/2$ – wait – $\pi/2$) turns phase into population. Its decaying fringes measure $T_2^*$, which includes reversible dephasing from unknown static frequency offsets.
- **Spin echo** (Hahn, 1950): a $\pi$ pulse at $\tau$ mirrors every phase and refocuses the spins at $2\tau$, undoing static offsets. What's left is the irreversible $T_2$. More pulses give **dynamical decoupling**.
- Coherence time ÷ gate time is about $10^3$–$10^5$ on today's hardware, which is far too few operations for big algorithms without error correction.
