Copying is the most basic operation in computing. `cp`, `git clone`, `memcpy`, backups and caches all depend on it, and it's so cheap we never think about it.

Imagine a **quantum photocopier**: put in any qubit $|\psi\rangle$ plus a blank qubit, and get two copies of $|\psi\rangle$ out. It would be enormously powerful.

- **Perfect state reading.** One measurement of a qubit only tells you one bit. With a copier you'd make a million copies, measure some along $x$, some along $y$ and some along $z$, and pin down $\theta$ and $\phi$ to many decimal places, all from a single original.
- **Faster-than-light messaging.** Bob's half of a Bell pair becomes $|0\rangle/|1\rangle$ if Alice measures in $Z$, or $|+\rangle/|-\rangle$ if she measures in $X$. If Bob could copy his qubit a thousand times and measure the copies in $Z$, all-equal results would mean "Z" and a random mix would mean "X". Lesson 3's no-signalling theorem would be dead.

## A copier that works, sometimes

Start with the obvious candidate. CNOT flips the target if the control is 1, so with a blank target $|0\rangle$:

$$
\text{CNOT}\,|0\rangle|0\rangle = |0\rangle|0\rangle, \qquad \text{CNOT}\,|1\rangle|0\rangle = |1\rangle|1\rangle .
$$

It copies **basis states** perfectly. It's the quantum version of `b = a` for a classical bit. Now feed it a superposition, $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$. By linearity,

$$
\text{CNOT}\,(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)|0\rangle = \alpha|00\rangle + \beta|11\rangle ,
$$

but a real copy would be

$$
|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle = \alpha^2|00\rangle + \alpha\beta|01\rangle + \alpha\beta|10\rangle + \beta^2|11\rangle .
$$

They match only when $\alpha\beta = 0$. For $|+\rangle$ the "copier" produces the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$, which is **entangled**, and that's about as different from $|+\rangle|+\rangle$ as a state can be. Explore all inputs:

## The proof: copying would break inner products

Maybe a cleverer circuit would work. It won't, and the proof uses only one fact: **unitaries preserve inner products**. If $|a'\rangle = U|a\rangle$ and $|b'\rangle = U|b\rangle$, then $\langle a'|b'\rangle = \langle a|U^\dagger U|b\rangle = \langle a|b\rangle$. Unitaries are rigid rotations: they never change the angle between two states.

Suppose a universal copier $U$ existed. Then for any two states $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$:

$$
U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle, \qquad U|\phi\rangle|0\rangle = |\phi\rangle|\phi\rangle .
$$

Take the inner product of the inputs and of the outputs. They must be equal:

$$
\underbrace{\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\,\langle 0|0\rangle}_{\text{before}} = \underbrace{\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\,\langle\psi|\phi\rangle}_{\text{after}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = x^2 \quad\text{where } x = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle .
$$

So $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ is $0$ or $1$. **A single device can only copy states that are identical or orthogonal.** Any two non-orthogonal, non-identical states, like $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$, defeat it.

<KeyIdea>
**No-cloning theorem:** no physical process can take an *unknown* quantum state $|\psi\rangle$ and output two copies $|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$. You can copy members of one orthogonal set (classical information), but never two states that overlap.
</KeyIdea>

<DeepDive title="Second proof: linearity, and why extra ancillas don't help">
Suppose $U|0\rangle|0\rangle|E\rangle = |0\rangle|0\rangle|E_0\rangle$ and $U|1\rangle|0\rangle|E\rangle = |1\rangle|1\rangle|E_1\rangle$, where we allow any number of extra "machine" qubits $|E\rangle$ that may end in whatever state they like. By linearity, the input $|+\rangle$ gives

$$
U|+\rangle|0\rangle|E\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|00\rangle|E_0\rangle + |11\rangle|E_1\rangle\big).
$$

That's not $|+\rangle|+\rangle|E_+\rangle$ for any $|E_+\rangle$, because the right side has no $|01\rangle$ or $|10\rangle$ terms, and $|++\rangle$ does. Adding machine qubits, measurements followed by post-processing, or general quantum channels changes nothing. The inner-product argument works for any of them, so no-cloning holds for every physical process.
</DeepDive>

## What *is* allowed

No-cloning is precise, so it's worth being precise about what it *doesn't* forbid.

- **Copying a known state.** If you know $\theta$ and $\phi$, just prepare a fresh copy. You're copying the *description*, not the qubit.
- **Copying classical information.** Bits written in one orthogonal basis copy fine. That's why classical computers built from quantum atoms work at all.
- **Moving a state.** A SWAP gate moves $|\psi\rangle$ from one qubit to another, and the source ends up in $|0\rangle$. Teleportation (World 8) moves a state across a distance, and it *destroys the original* in the process. Quantum information can be moved but not duplicated.
- **Imperfect copies.** You can make two *approximate* copies. The best universal $1\to2$ cloner, found by **Bužek and Hillery (1996)**, gives each copy fidelity $\tfrac56 \approx 0.833$ with the original, for every input state. The CNOT copier only manages $\tfrac23$ on average, and only $\tfrac12$ on the equator.

</NumQuiz>

<Warning>
"No-cloning means quantum states are fragile or secret." **Not exactly.** A qubit isn't hidden from you, and you can measure it any time you like. What you can't do is learn *everything* about one unknown copy, because measuring disturbs it and you can't make spares to measure separately. The restriction is on extracting complete information from a single instance. That restriction is what quantum cryptography uses.
</Warning>

## Consequences everywhere

**It protects relativity.** The faster-than-light scheme at the top of this lesson needed exactly one impossible ingredient, a copier. No-cloning and no-signalling are two faces of the same structure.

**It makes quantum key distribution secure.** In BB84 (World 8), an eavesdropper would love to copy each photon, forward the original, and measure her copy later once the bases are announced. No-cloning forbids it. Any attempt to learn about the photons disturbs them, and that disturbance shows up as errors Alice and Bob can detect.

**It makes error correction harder, but not impossible.** Classical error correction starts by copying: send 000 instead of 0. Quantum codes can't copy $|\psi\rangle$, but they can **entangle**. They encode $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle \mapsto \alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle$ using two CNOTs. That's exactly the "failed copier" from this lesson, put to work. World 11 picks up the story.

**It forces teleportation to be destructive.** When a state arrives at Bob's end, Alice's original has necessarily been scrambled. Otherwise there would be two copies.

<CSCorner>
In classical systems copying is free, and whole architectures are built on that: caches, replication, snapshots, `fork()`. Quantum information follows a **linear (affine) type discipline** instead. A qubit value can be moved or consumed, but not duplicated. Rust's ownership model, where a non-`Copy` value can be moved but not implicitly duplicated, is a good analogy. Quantum programming languages like Q# and Silq enforce this: you can't write `let b = a;` for a qubit and keep using both. Where the analogy breaks is that a quantum program can still *entangle* `a` with a fresh qubit (CNOT), which looks like a copy in the computational basis but isn't one in any other.
</CSCorner>

<History>
In 1982 the physicist **Nick Herbert** proposed "FLASH", a scheme for faster-than-light communication using entangled photons and an idealised laser amplifier that would duplicate incoming photons. Working out precisely why it must fail produced the no-cloning theorem, published that same year by **William Wootters and Wojciech Zurek** ("A single quantum cannot be cloned", *Nature*) and independently by **Dennis Dieks**. (James Park had essentially proved it in 1970, but nobody noticed.) A flawed paper that led to a fundamental theorem was a good outcome.
</History>

## Summary

- CNOT copies $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. On a superposition it **entangles** instead: $(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)|0\rangle \mapsto \alpha|00\rangle + \beta|11\rangle \ne |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$.
- **No-cloning:** unitaries preserve inner products, and copying would force $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2$. So only identical or orthogonal states can be copied by one device.
- Allowed: re-preparing known states, copying classical (orthogonal) information, moving states (SWAP, teleportation), and approximate cloning (best universal fidelity $\tfrac56$).
- Consequences: no FTL signalling, security of quantum cryptography, error correction by entangling rather than copying, and teleportation that destroys the original.
