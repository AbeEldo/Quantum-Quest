In 1969, a young physicist named **John Clauser** read Bell's paper and realised nobody had actually *checked*. He wrote to Bell, Bohm and de Broglie asking whether an experiment was worth doing. Most physicists thought it wasn't: quantum mechanics had never been wrong, and the question seemed like philosophy. Clauser later recalled that the work was not good for his career.

He did it anyway. In 1972, at Berkeley, **Stuart Freedman and John Clauser** sent pairs of entangled photons from excited calcium atoms through two polarisers and counted coincidences for days.

## The three loopholes

Bell's derivation assumes an ideal experiment. Real experiments must make sure a local hidden-variable model can't sneak through any of these gaps.

### 1. Locality (communication)

If information about Alice's setting could reach Bob's apparatus before his outcome is recorded, even by an unknown mechanism travelling at light speed, a local model could cheat. The fix is to make the setting choice and the distant measurement **spacelike separated**: each outcome must be recorded within a time $\tau$ shorter than the light-travel time $d/c$ between the labs.

<Try>

- Load *Delft 2015*: 1.3 km gives about $4.3\ \mu s$ of light-travel time. The fast random choice plus spin readout fit inside it.
</Try>

### 2. Detection (fair sampling)

Photon detectors miss a lot of photons. If you only analyse the pairs where *both* photons were detected, you're assuming the detected subset is a fair sample. A clever local model could break that assumption: its particles "decide" not to be detected when the setting is unfavourable, and the surviving events then show fake Bell violations.

<DetectionLoophole />

For the CHSH test with maximally entangled states, the overall efficiency must exceed $2(\sqrt2-1)\approx 82.8\%$. Eberhard showed in 1993 that cleverly chosen *partially* entangled states lower the requirement to $2/3$. For decades, photon detectors were far below either threshold, around 5 to 30% end to end.

### 3. Freedom of choice (measurement independence)

Bell assumes the settings are independent of the hidden variables $\lambda$. If the "random" choices were secretly correlated with the source, any correlation could be faked. Experiments use fast quantum random-number generators, human choices (the 2016 **BIG Bell Test** collected input from about 100,000 volunteers playing an online game), and even the colour of light from distant **quasars**, emitted billions of years ago. Then any conspiracy would have to be set up before the Earth existed. This loophole can never be closed *completely*. A fully **superdeterministic** universe, where every choice is preordained along with every particle, can't be ruled out by any experiment. Most physicists regard that as a reductio rather than a theory.

## 2015: loophole-free

In autumn 2015, three groups independently closed locality and detection in the same experiment.

- **Delft (Hensen et al., *Nature*):** two nitrogen-vacancy centres, single electron spins in diamond, in labs **1.3 km** apart on the TU Delft campus. Each spin emitted a photon, and the photons met at a station in between. When a particular joint detection occurred there, the distant spins were entangled. This **event-ready** scheme heralds the good runs in advance, so lost photons don't open a detection loophole, and spin readout is nearly perfect. Over 245 trials they found $S = 2.42 \pm 0.20$, with a $p$-value of about 0.04.
- **Vienna (Giustina et al.) and NIST Boulder (Shalm et al.)**, published together in *Physical Review Letters*: entangled photons with superconducting detectors efficient enough to beat the detection threshold, and random settings spacelike separated from the distant measurement. Much higher event rates gave overwhelming statistics. Vienna reported $p \approx 3.7\times10^{-31}$.

## What it rules out, and what it doesn't

The verdict, from many experiments with many technologies (photons, atoms, ions, electron spins, superconducting circuits), is clear: **nature violates Bell inequalities**. So no theory satisfying all of Bell's assumptions can describe the world:

- **Realism / hidden variables:** outcomes are determined by pre-existing properties $\lambda$.
- **Locality:** no influence on an outcome from events outside its past light cone.
- **Freedom of choice:** the settings are independent of $\lambda$.

The usual summary is that **local realism is dead**. Which assumption to give up is a question of interpretation, and physicists still argue about it.

<Warning>
Common overstatements to avoid:

- "Bell tests prove that particles communicate instantly." They show the correlations can't be explained by *local* pre-existing values. They don't show a usable signal, and no-signalling is itself a theorem of quantum mechanics.
- "Bell tests prove quantum mechanics." Experiments falsify theories. They can't prove one. What they prove is that *any* correct theory must be nonlocal in Bell's sense, or give up realism or free choice.
- "The loopholes mean the question is still open." Every major loophole has been closed, all together in single experiments. What remains is superdeterminism, which can't be tested by construction.
</Warning>

<CSCorner>
Loophole-free Bell tests now power applications. **Device-independent randomness:** NIST has run a public randomness beacon whose bits are certified random by a loophole-free Bell violation. If the bits were predictable, local realism would hold. **Device-independent key distribution:** experiments in 2022 (trapped ions in Oxford, neutral atoms in Munich) generated secret keys whose security rests on a Bell violation rather than on trusting the hardware. In engineering terms, the Bell test has gone from a foundations experiment to a self-testing **certificate**.
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- **1972, Freedman & Clauser:** first Bell test, violated. **1982, Aspect:** settings switched in flight, $S \approx 2.70$. **1998, Weihs/Zeilinger:** strict locality with random settings. **2001, NIST ions:** detection loophole closed.
- **Loopholes:** *locality* (outcome recorded within $\tau < d/c$), *detection* (efficiency above ≈82.8% for CHSH, or 2/3 with Eberhard's trick), *freedom of choice* (random, human or cosmic setting choices, never fully closable against superdeterminism).
- **2015:** Delft (NV spins 1.3 km apart, $S = 2.42\pm0.20$), Vienna and NIST (photons) close locality and detection together.
- **2022 Nobel Prize:** Aspect, Clauser, Zeilinger.
- **Ruled out:** local realism. **Not implied:** faster-than-light signalling, or any particular interpretation.
