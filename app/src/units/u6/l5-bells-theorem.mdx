In 1935, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen published a four-page paper with a provocative title: *"Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?"* Their answer was **no**.

Their argument used exactly the correlations you met in Lesson 3. Here it is in modern dress. Alice and Bob share $|\Phi^+\rangle$, far apart.

- If Alice measures along $Z$, she can predict Bob's $Z$ result **with certainty**, without touching his particle.
- If she measures along $X$ instead, she can predict Bob's $X$ result with certainty.

EPR's **criterion of reality**: *"If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty … the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to this physical quantity."* Nothing Alice does can disturb Bob's distant particle (locality). So Bob's particle must *already carry* definite answers for both $Z$ and $X$. Quantum mechanics has no place for both answers at once, so it must be **incomplete**, and some deeper theory with "hidden variables" should fill in the gaps.

## What a local hidden-variable theory is

Give EPR everything they want. A **local hidden-variable (LHV) model** says:

1. **Realism / hidden variables:** at the source, each pair is assigned some data $\lambda$, drawn from any probability distribution you like. You can think of $\lambda$ as a slip of paper with instructions.
2. **Locality:** Alice's outcome depends only on $\lambda$ and *her own* setting $a$, written $A(a,\lambda)$. Bob's depends only on $\lambda$ and his setting, $B(b,\lambda)$. Neither outcome can depend on the other side's setting.
3. **Free choice:** the settings $a, b$ are chosen independently of $\lambda$. The source doesn't know in advance which questions will be asked.

That's a very general class. $\lambda$ can be anything: real numbers, a whole computer program, a random seed. In CS terms, Alice and Bob are two *deterministic programs* that may share a random string generated at the source but can't communicate after the questions arrive. (Randomness inside each program adds nothing new, since you can fold it into $\lambda$.)

<Quiz id="u6l5-lhv">
  <Q>Which of these would break the "locality" assumption of an LHV model?</Q>
  <Opt>The source gives both particles a long random string</Opt>
  <Opt correct>Bob's outcome is allowed to depend on Alice's analyser setting</Opt>
  <Opt>Alice's outcome is generated by a pseudorandom function of $\lambda$ and $a$</Opt>
  <Opt>$\lambda$ is drawn from a very complicated distribution</Opt>
  <Explain>Shared strings, complicated distributions and pseudorandomness are all allowed, because they're part of $\lambda$. The only thing forbidden is information about the *other* side's setting reaching your outcome. That would take a signal between the labs, and experiments arrange things so no light-speed signal has time to arrive.</Explain>
</Quiz>

## Mermin's device

Physicist **N. David Mermin** found a version of Bell's argument you can check with simple counting. Two detectors each have a 3-position switch and a light that flashes red or green. A source between them emits a pair, each side picks a setting at random *after* emission, and the lights flash. You observe two facts:

- **Fact 1:** when the switches are in the *same* position, the lights *always* agree.
- **Fact 2:** averaged over all random settings, the lights agree only **half** the time.

Quantum mechanics produces exactly this. Use $|\Phi^+\rangle$ photons and analyser angles $0°, 60°, 120°$. Same setting gives agreement 1. Different settings give $\cos^2 60° = \tfrac14$. Averaged: $\tfrac13\cdot 1 + \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac12$.

**Your job:** reproduce both facts with instruction sets. Fact 1 forces each pair to carry the *same* set of three colours, one per setting (otherwise equal settings could disagree). So you choose how often the source prints each of the 8 sets RRR, RRG, … GGG.

- Try any mixture you like. The overall agreement never drops below **55.6%**. Then switch to the entangled source: 50%.

Quantum mechanics gives 50%. **This is a Bell inequality:** $P(\text{agree}) \ge 5/9$ for every local hidden-variable model, violated by entangled particles.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

## The CHSH inequality

Experiments use a cleaner, more robust version due to **Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt (1969)**. Alice picks one of two settings $a$ or $a'$, and Bob picks $b$ or $b'$. Each outcome is $\pm1$. Define the **correlator**

$$
E(a,b) = \langle A\,B\rangle = P(\text{same}) - P(\text{different}) ,
$$

and the CHSH combination

$$
S = E(a,b) + E(a,b') + E(a',b) - E(a',b') .
$$

**The local bound.** For a single pair with hidden instructions $\lambda$, the four answers $A(a), A(a'), B(b), B(b')$ are fixed numbers $\pm1$. Then

$$
A(a)\big[B(b) + B(b')\big] + A(a')\big[B(b) - B(b')\big] = \pm 2,
$$

because one bracket is 0 and the other is $\pm2$. $S$ is the average of this quantity over $\lambda$, and an average of numbers that are each $\pm2$ lies between them:

$$
\boxed{\;|S| \le 2\;} \qquad\text{for every local hidden-variable model.}
$$

**The quantum prediction.** For $|\Phi^+\rangle$ photons, $P(\text{same}) = \cos^2(\theta_A - \theta_B)$, so $E = \cos^2\Delta - \sin^2\Delta = \cos 2(\theta_A - \theta_B)$. Choose $a = 0°$, $a' = 45°$, $b = 22.5°$, $b' = -22.5°$. Three pairs are $22.5°$ apart and give $E = \cos 45° = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, and the pair $(a', b')$ is $67.5°$ apart and gives $E = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$:

$$
S = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} - \big(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big) = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83 .
$$

## Tsirelson's bound and beyond

Quantum mechanics beats 2, but it doesn't reach the algebraic maximum of 4. **Boris Tsirelson (1980)** proved that no quantum state and no measurements can exceed

$$
|S| \le 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.828 .
$$

Here's a sketch of why. With $\pm1$-valued observables, $\mathcal{S}^2 = 4I - [A, A'][B, B']$, and each commutator has norm at most 2, so $\|\mathcal S\|^2 \le 8$. Curiously, $S = 4$ wouldn't violate relativity either. Popescu and Rohrlich (1994) described hypothetical "PR boxes" that reach $S = 4$ and still can't signal. Why nature stops at $2\sqrt2$ is still an active research question.

| model | max $\lvert S\rvert$ | in words |
|---|---|---|
| local hidden variables | 2 | instructions fixed at the source |
| quantum mechanics | $2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$ | entangled pairs (Tsirelson) |
| no-signalling "PR box" | 4 | hypothetical, not seen in nature |

<NumQuiz id="u6l5-s-value" answer={2.598} tol={0.02}>
  <Q>Quantum photons, but with Alice's settings $a = 0°$, $a' = 30°$ and Bob's $b = 15°$, $b' = -15°$. What is $S$? (Use $E = \cos 2(\theta_A - \theta_B)$.)</Q>
  <Hint>Three of the setting pairs are $15°$ apart. The pair $(a', b')$ is $45°$ apart.</Hint>
  <Explain>$E(a,b) = E(a,b') = E(a',b) = \cos 30° \approx 0.866$, and $E(a',b') = \cos 90° = 0$. So $S = 3(0.866) - 0 \approx 2.598$. That beats the local bound of 2, but not by as much as the optimal angles do. With equal spacing $\delta$ between neighbours, $S = 3\cos 2\delta - \cos 6\delta$, which peaks at $\delta = 22.5°$ with $S = 2\sqrt2$.</Explain>
</NumQuiz>

<Warning>
"Bell's theorem proves that quantum particles send signals to each other faster than light." **No.** Bell's theorem says that at least one of the assumptions (realism, locality, free choice) fails. It says nothing about usable signals, and Lesson 3 showed there aren't any. Different interpretations give up different assumptions. Bohmian mechanics keeps definite values but is explicitly nonlocal. Many-worlds denies that measurements have single outcomes. Others reject "realism" about unmeasured quantities. What no interpretation can keep is **local realism** in EPR's sense.
</Warning>

<CSCorner>
Bell's theorem is a statement about **distributed computation without communication**. Two non-communicating machines, even with an unlimited shared random tape, can produce only "local" correlations, whose possible statistics form a polytope with 16 deterministic corner points. The CHSH inequality is one face of that polytope. Entangled qubits let you produce correlations outside the polytope, still without communication. Next lesson turns this into a game you can win more often with entanglement than with any program.
</CSCorner>

<History>
**John Stewart Bell**, a Northern Irish physicist at CERN, wrote "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen paradox" while on leave in the United States. It appeared in 1964 in *Physics*, a short-lived journal few people read. Bell later admitted he had hoped to vindicate Einstein. In 1969 **John Clauser, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony and Richard Holt** reworked the inequality into the CHSH form that real, imperfect detectors could test. Next stop: the experiments.
</History>

## Summary

- **EPR (1935):** perfect correlations plus locality imply pre-existing values, so quantum mechanics must be incomplete.
- **Local hidden variables:** outcomes $A(a,\lambda), B(b,\lambda)$ fixed by shared data $\lambda$, with settings chosen freely.
- **Mermin's device:** any instruction-set model agrees at least $5/9$ of the time, while entangled pairs agree $1/2$ of the time.
- **CHSH:** $S = E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b')$ satisfies $|S|\le 2$ for every LHV model. Quantum mechanics reaches $2\sqrt2$ (Tsirelson's bound) at angles $0°, 45°$ vs $\pm22.5°$.
- **Bell's theorem:** no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce quantum mechanics. Experiment has to decide, and it has.
