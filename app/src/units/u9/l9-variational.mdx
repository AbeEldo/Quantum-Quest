Everything so far, including Shor, phase estimation and big Grover searches, needs long, deep circuits and therefore **error correction**: thousands of logical qubits made from perhaps a million physical ones. Today's machines have roughly 100 to 1,000+ physical qubits, two-qubit gate errors around 0.1–1%, and no large-scale error correction. After a few thousand gates, noise wins.

John Preskill named this era **NISQ** (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) in 2018. The question it raises: *can short, noisy circuits do anything useful?* The leading idea is a **hybrid** loop. A small quantum circuit prepares a parameterized state and measures something about it, and a classical computer adjusts the parameters.

## The variational principle

Expand any state in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_j c_j|E_j\rangle$ with $E_0 \le E_1 \le \cdots$. Then
$$
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle = \sum_j |c_j|^2 E_j \ \ge\ \sum_j |c_j|^2 E_0 = E_0 ,
$$
with equality exactly when $|\psi\rangle$ is a ground state. The energy is a weighted average of eigenvalues, and an average can't be below the minimum.

That turns "find the ground state" into "minimize a function". Choose a family of states $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ (the **ansatz**) and minimize $E(\vec\theta) = \langle\psi(\vec\theta)|H|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ over $\vec\theta$.

## VQE: the loop

The **variational quantum eigensolver** (Peruzzo et al., 2014):

1. **Ansatz:** a short parameterized circuit, e.g. layers of $R_y(\theta_k)$ rotations and CNOTs.
2. **Measure the energy.** Write $H$ as a sum of Pauli strings, like $H = Z\otimes Z + 0.6(X\otimes I + I\otimes X)$. Measure each term by rotating into its basis and averaging $\pm1$ outcomes over many shots.
3. **Classical optimizer:** update $\vec\theta$ to lower $E$, and repeat.

The quantum computer does what's hard classically: preparing and measuring a highly entangled state whose energy you couldn't compute directly. The classical computer does the rest.

## Gradients from the quantum computer: the parameter-shift rule

How does the optimizer get gradients when all it can do is estimate $E(\vec\theta)$ from samples? Finite differences are terrible with shot noise: dividing a noisy difference by a tiny step blows up the noise. Instead, for gates of the form $e^{-i\theta P/2}$ with $P$ a Pauli ($R_x, R_y, R_z$), there's an **exact** formula:
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial\theta_k} = \frac{E(\theta_k + \tfrac{\pi}{2}) - E(\theta_k - \tfrac{\pi}{2})}{2}.
$$

<DeepDive title="Why the shift rule is exact">
Fix all parameters except $\theta = \theta_k$. Because $e^{-i\theta P/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\, I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,P$ and $P^2 = I$, the energy is a trigonometric polynomial of degree 1 in $\theta$: $E(\theta) = A + B\cos\theta + C\sin\theta$. For such a function, $E'(\theta) = -B\sin\theta + C\cos\theta$. And $\tfrac12\big[E(\theta+\tfrac\pi2) - E(\theta-\tfrac\pi2)\big] = \tfrac12\big[-2B\sin\theta + 2C\cos\theta\big]$, which is the same. (This is the tangent of a sinusoid measured from two points a half-period apart.) The test suite in this unit checks the formula against finite differences.
</DeepDive>

## QAOA: variational circuits for optimization

The **quantum approximate optimization algorithm** (Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann, 2014) applies the same loop to combinatorial optimization. Take **Max-Cut**: color the vertices of a graph with two colors so that as many edges as possible connect different colors. Encode a coloring as a bit string $z$ and define the cost
$$
C(z) = \sum_{(u,v)\in E} [z_u \neq z_v] .
$$
QAOA with depth $p$ prepares
$$
|\gamma,\beta\rangle = e^{-i\beta_p B}e^{-i\gamma_p C}\cdots e^{-i\beta_1 B}e^{-i\gamma_1 C}\,|+\rangle^{\otimes n}, \qquad B = \sum_v X_v ,
$$
alternating a **phase separator** $e^{-i\gamma C}$ (which phases each bit string by its cut value: a diagonal, phase-oracle-like step) with a **mixer** $e^{-i\beta B}$ (which lets amplitude flow between bit strings and interfere). Then it tunes the $2p$ angles to maximize $\langle C\rangle$.

## The catch: barren plateaus, noise, and no proven advantage

<Warning title="Variational algorithms are heuristics, with serious open problems">
- **Barren plateaus** (McClean et al., 2018). For sufficiently random, deep ansatz circuits, gradients vanish *exponentially* in the number of qubits: the landscape is flat almost everywhere, and estimating a gradient to useful precision needs exponentially many shots. Hardware noise causes its own barren plateaus (Wang et al., 2021). Avoiding them requires structured, problem-aware ansätze, and recent work suggests that landscapes provably free of barren plateaus are often efficiently *classically simulable*.
- **Measurement cost.** Reaching precision $\epsilon$ takes about $1/\epsilon^2$ shots per term. Chemical accuracy across thousands of Pauli terms can mean billions of circuit runs.
- **Noise** biases the energy, and error mitigation adds overhead that grows exponentially with circuit size.
- **No proven speedup.** QAOA at $p = 1$ guarantees a cut ratio of 0.6924 on 3-regular graphs, while the classical Goemans–Williamson algorithm guarantees 0.878 on every graph. Whether VQE or QAOA beats the best classical methods on any practically relevant instance is open, and so far careful classical benchmarks have kept pace.
</Warning>

<History>
**Peruzzo et al. (2014)** ran the first VQE on a two-qubit photonic chip, finding the ground-state energy of the HeH⁺ molecular ion. **IBM (Kandala et al., 2017)** scaled "hardware-efficient" VQE to molecules like BeH₂ on six superconducting qubits. **QAOA** was introduced by Edward Farhi, Jeffrey Goldstone and Sam Gutmann in 2014, and for a short time in 2014–15 it held the best known approximation ratio for a particular constraint-satisfaction problem, until a better classical algorithm appeared. That episode is a miniature of the whole field's quantum-vs-classical back-and-forth.
</History>

<CSCorner>
Think of VQE and QAOA as **training a model whose forward pass runs on quantum hardware**. Everything ML engineers know applies: the choice of architecture (ansatz) is an inductive bias, optimizers get stuck in local minima, gradients can vanish, and evaluation is noisy and expensive. The difference is that each "forward pass" costs thousands of hardware shots. And unlike neural networks, nobody yet has a striking empirical success that justifies the approach at scale.
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- **NISQ** devices can't run Shor-sized circuits, so **hybrid variational algorithms** use short parameterized circuits inside a classical optimization loop.
- **Variational principle:** $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\ge E_0$, so minimizing the energy over an ansatz gives an upper bound on the ground energy.
- **VQE:** ansatz → measure Pauli terms → classical optimizer. Gradients come from the **parameter-shift rule**, $\partial_\theta E = \tfrac12[E(\theta+\tfrac\pi2) - E(\theta-\tfrac\pi2)]$.
- **QAOA:** alternate the cost phase $e^{-i\gamma C}$ and the mixer $e^{-i\beta\sum X}$, then tune the angles to maximize the expected cut.
- **Caveats:** barren plateaus, huge measurement costs, noise, and **no proven advantage** over the best classical algorithms.
