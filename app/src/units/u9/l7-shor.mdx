Multiplying two 1024-bit primes takes a laptop microseconds. Going backwards, factoring their 2048-bit product, is believed to be out of reach for every classical computer on Earth. The record for a hard RSA-style number is **RSA-250** (829 bits), factored in 2020 with roughly 2,700 CPU core-years of computation. Each additional ~10 digits makes it dramatically harder. The security of RSA, the backbone of internet key exchange and signatures for decades, rests on this asymmetry.

In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could factor an $n$-bit number in time **polynomial** in $n$. Discrete logarithms (Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography) fall to the same idea.

## Step 1: factoring reduces to order finding

Shor's algorithm is mostly classical number theory with one quantum subroutine. Let $N$ be odd and composite (and not a prime power; both cases are easy to detect and handle classically).

1. Pick a random $a$ with $1 < a < N$. If $\gcd(a, N) > 1$, you've found a factor by luck (Euclid's algorithm is fast).
2. Otherwise, find the **order** $r$ of $a$ modulo $N$: the smallest $r > 0$ with $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$. **This is the quantum step.**
3. If $r$ is even, then $a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) \equiv 0 \pmod N$. So $N$ divides the product. If additionally $a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1$, then $N$ divides neither factor (it can't divide $a^{r/2}-1$, or $r$ wouldn't be minimal), so $N$ must share a nontrivial factor with each:
$$
p = \gcd(a^{r/2} - 1,\ N), \qquad q = \gcd(a^{r/2} + 1,\ N).
$$
4. For a random $a$, the probability that $r$ is even *and* $a^{r/2}\not\equiv -1$ is at least ½ (Nielsen & Chuang, Thm 5.3). If you're unlucky, pick another $a$.

**Worked example: $N = 15$, $a = 7$.**

| $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $7^x \bmod 15$ | 1 | 7 | 4 | 13 | 1 | 7 | 4 |

The sequence repeats with period $r = 4$, which is even. $7^{2} = 49 \equiv 4 \pmod{15}$, and $4 \not\equiv -1$. So
$$
\gcd(7^2 - 1, 15) = \gcd(48, 15) = 3, \qquad \gcd(7^2 + 1, 15) = \gcd(50, 15) = 5 .
$$
$15 = 3\times 5$. ✓

## Step 2: order finding is period finding

Order finding is hard classically because the period $r$ can be as large as $N$, exponential in the bit length. But $f(x) = a^x \bmod N$ is a **periodic function**, $f(x + r) = f(x)$, and finding hidden periods is exactly what the Fourier transform is good at.

The quantum subroutine:

1. Counting register of $t$ qubits (with $Q = 2^t \ge N^2$), work register of $\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ qubits set to $|1\rangle$. Hadamard the counting register.
2. Compute $|x\rangle|1\rangle \mapsto |x\rangle|a^x \bmod N\rangle$ in superposition. This is **modular exponentiation**, built from controlled multiplications by $a^{2^k} \bmod N$ (precomputed classically by repeated squaring). That's phase estimation's "efficient controlled-$U^{2^k}$".
3. (Conceptually) measure the work register. You see some value $f(x_0)$, and the counting register collapses to the **comb** $|x_0\rangle + |x_0 + r\rangle + |x_0 + 2r\rangle + \cdots$, with a random offset.
4. Apply $\mathrm{QFT}^{-1}$ and measure. The peaks sit near multiples of $Q/r$, whatever the offset.

The phase-estimation view says the same thing. $U_a|w\rangle = |aw \bmod N\rangle$ has eigenvalues $e^{2\pi i k/r}$, and $|1\rangle$ is a uniform superposition of its eigenvectors. Phase estimation returns $y/Q \approx k/r$ for a uniformly random $k$.

## Step 3: continued fractions turn a peak into $r$

A measurement gives $y$ with
$$
\left|\frac{y}{Q} - \frac{k}{r}\right| \le \frac{1}{2Q} \le \frac{1}{2N^2} < \frac{1}{2r^2}
$$
for some unknown $k$. A classical theorem (Legendre's) says any fraction $k/r$ that close to $y/Q$ **must appear among the convergents** of the continued-fraction expansion of $y/Q$. That's why we took $Q \ge N^2$. So expand $y/Q$, list the convergents $p/q$ with $q < N$, and test each denominator: is $a^q \equiv 1 \pmod N$?

**Worked example: $N = 21$, $a = 2$, $Q = 512$, measured $y = 427$.**
$$
\frac{427}{512} = 0 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{5 + \cfrac{1}{42 + \cfrac{1}{2}}}} = [0;\,1, 5, 42, 2].
$$
The convergents are $\tfrac01, \tfrac11, \tfrac56, \tfrac{211}{253}, \dots$. The last denominator below 21 is $6$, and $2^6 = 64 \equiv 1 \pmod{21}$ ✓. So $r = 6$, and $\gcd(2^3 \pm 1, 21)$ gives $7$ and $3$.

Click a peak in the widget (or press measure), then click the convergents to test them.

<Note title="When it doesn't work the first time">
If the random $k$ shares a factor with $r$ (say $k = 2$, $r = 6$), the convergent is $k/r$ in lowest terms, $1/3$, and its denominator is only a divisor of $r$. Testing small multiples of the denominator, or taking the LCM of the denominators from two runs, fixes this. With $y = 0$ you learn nothing. Overall a constant number of runs suffices on average.
</Note>

## What would it take to break RSA-2048?

The expensive part is the modular exponentiation: $O(n^3)$ gates with schoolbook arithmetic (less with faster multiplication) on roughly $2n$ to $3n$ **logical** qubits. Logical qubits are error-corrected (World 11); each one is built from hundreds to thousands of physical qubits. The headline estimates, all for RSA-2048 with physical error rates around $10^{-3}$:

| estimate | physical qubits | runtime | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gidney & Ekerå (2019) | ~20 million | ~8 hours | surface code; the long-standing benchmark |
| Gidney (2025) | under 1 million | under a week | approximate arithmetic, yoked surface codes, magic-state cultivation |
| Pinnacle architecture, Iceberg Quantum (2026 preprint) | ~100,000 | ~1 month | quantum LDPC codes; not yet peer-reviewed, assumes fast long-range connectivity |

For scale: the largest superconducting chips in 2024–2026 have on the order of 100 to 1,000+ physical qubits (Google's Willow has 105; IBM's Condor has 1,121), and logical-qubit demonstrations involve a handful to dozens of logical qubits. Peer-reviewed estimates fell about 20-fold between 2019 and 2025, preprints claim more, and hardware keeps growing. Nobody can give a reliable date, but the trend is why cryptographers aren't waiting.

<Warning title='"Harvest now, decrypt later"'>
An adversary can record encrypted traffic **today** and decrypt it once a large quantum computer exists. Data that must stay secret for 10+ years is already at risk. That's why NIST standardized post-quantum algorithms in 2024 (ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA, based on lattices and hash functions), and its transition plan (NIST IR 8547) deprecates RSA and elliptic-curve public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallows it after 2035. Symmetric crypto (AES) and hash functions are only mildly affected (Grover gives a square root, not a break).
</Warning>

## The experimental record, and its caveats

<History>
- **2001, IBM Almaden and Stanford (Vandersypen et al., *Nature*)**: factored 15 using 7 nuclear spins in a liquid-state NMR molecule. A landmark of control, but NMR works with huge ensembles of molecules in highly mixed states that can't be scaled up, and the circuit was simplified using knowledge of the answer.
- **2012 (Martín-López et al.)**: factored 21 with photons and "qubit recycling", again with a compiled, answer-aware circuit.
- **2013, Smolin, Smith & Vargo, *Oversimplifying quantum factoring***: showed that such compiled circuits can be shrunk until "factoring" any $N$ needs as little as a coin flip, so they demonstrate quantum control rather than factoring.
- **2016 (Monz et al., *Science*, trapped ions)**: factored 15 with an implementation designed to avoid answer-specific shortcuts.

Every Shor demonstration so far fits on a napkin. Headlines about factoring larger numbers "with a quantum computer" have used other methods (annealing or variational heuristics) with no proven scaling, and they are not Shor's algorithm.
</History>

<CSCorner>
Complexity-wise, factoring is in $\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ (certificates exist for both "has a factor below $k$" and its negation) and in $\mathsf{BQP}$. It is **not believed NP-complete**: an NP-complete problem in coNP would imply NP = coNP. Its classical status is open. The best known algorithm, the general number field sieve, runs in $\exp\!\big(O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\big)$, but nobody has proved factoring isn't in P. So Shor's algorithm is an exponential speedup over the best **known** classical algorithm, not a proven separation.
</CSCorner>

## Summary

- **Factoring → order finding:** pick $a$; if the order $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv -1$, then $\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1, N)$ are factors. A random $a$ works with probability at least ½.
- Example: $N = 15$, $a = 7$ gives $r = 4$, then $7^2 = 49 \equiv 4$, and $\gcd(3, 15) = 3$, $\gcd(5, 15) = 5$.
- **Order finding is period finding** of $a^x \bmod N$: superpose, modular-exponentiate (efficient controlled-$U^{2^k}$), apply $\mathrm{QFT}^{-1}$, and measure $y \approx kQ/r$.
- **Continued fractions** of $y/Q$ (with $Q \ge N^2$) recover $k/r$ and hence $r$.
- RSA-2048 estimates have dropped from ~20 million (2019) to under 1 million (2025) physical qubits, with preprints going lower. Demonstrations remain at 15 and 21. Migration to **post-quantum cryptography** is under way.
